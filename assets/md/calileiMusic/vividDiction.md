# Vivid Diction — Choosing Words by Their Effect

*Which words you pick — for image, memory, and feeling — apart from how they sound.*

`phoneticAppeal.md` designs how a line *sounds*; this document designs what its words *mean and evoke*. They are the two halves of word choice — the sound side and the sense side — and a line needs both. A lyric can be rhythmically true (`prosodyMeter.md`), well-rhymed (`rhymingWords.md`), and sweet in the mouth (`phoneticAppeal.md`) and still land soft, because the words themselves are abstract, flat, and forgettable. Diction is the lever that decides whether a line **shows a picture, sticks in memory, and moves someone**.

Three forces drive nearly every word choice below: how **picturable** a word is, how **emotionally charged** it is, and how **fresh**. These are axes, not verdicts — where a given writer habitually sets them is that writer's *voice* (see `voiceStyle.md`, which sets the dials this document defines).

## 1. Concreteness: the picturable word

The single most reliable lever in diction: **concrete, picturable words are remembered far better than abstractions.** *Kettle, rust, headlights, gravel* stick; *sadness, freedom, closure, pain* slide off.

The mechanism is **dual coding** (Allan Paivio, 1971/1986). The mind runs two systems — a verbal one and an imagery one. A concrete word is stored *twice*, once as language and once as a mental image, so it has two independent retrieval routes; an abstraction gets only the verbal trace. The result, called the **concreteness effect**, is one of the most replicated findings in all of memory research: concrete nouns are recalled roughly *twice as well* as abstract nouns matched for length and frequency. *Truck* beats *truth*; *bicycle* beats *justice*. (Its cousin, the **picture-superiority effect**, is why an actual image out-sticks any word.)

This is the cognitive backbone of the oldest rule in the craft — **show, don't tell.** Naming an emotion gives the listener one thin verbal trace. Showing a concrete image that *implies* the emotion gives them a picture they can see, store, and refill with their own memory. *"The kettle's gone cold"* lands harder than *"I feel lonely,"* and it does so precisely because it never says *lonely*.

A practical note: if you ever need to gauge how concrete a word actually is, large rated norms exist (Brysbaert and colleagues scored tens of thousands of English words for concreteness) — but the ear test is usually enough: *can you photograph it?* If not, you are probably telling.

## 2. Object writing and the seven senses

If §1 says *use concrete images*, this is the method that *generates* them. **Object writing** — Pat Pattison's term (*Writing Better Lyrics*, 1995), the same lineage behind `rhymingWords.md` and `prosodyMeter.md` — is sense-bound free association: pick a thing or a moment and pour out everything the senses register, without editing. It is the mine the concrete words come from.

Pattison counts **seven** senses, not five:

- **Sight** — color, shape, light.
- **Sound** — what you hear.
- **Smell** — the most memory-laden of all, the fastest route to nostalgia.
- **Taste** — flavor, often paired with smell.
- **Touch** — texture, temperature, weight.
- **Organic** — awareness of the *inside* of the body: heartbeat, breath, the tight throat, the dropping stomach, nausea.
- **Kinesthetic** — the body's motion and its relation to space: falling, spinning, leaning, the floor tilting, the pull of a stopped car.

The last two are the ones writers forget, and they are where the most visceral, least clichéd images live — because everyone reaches for sight first, so sight is where the clichés pile up. A grief written through the **organic** sense (a breath that won't fill) or the **kinesthetic** sense (the ground not quite holding) is fresher than a thousand grey skies.

Why it works on the listener and not just the writer: when a lyric fires someone's senses, **they fill your words with their own experience** — their kitchen, their winter, their slammed door. The song becomes about them. Sense-bound writing pulls a listener in through their own body and memory, which no statement of feeling can do. So when a line drifts abstract, run the moment back through the seven senses and pull the one or two concrete details that imply the whole — and reach past sight.

## 3. Making the abstract concrete: metaphor

Sometimes the subject simply *is* abstract — love, grief, time, faith, addiction. You cannot photograph grief. **Metaphor is the bridge that makes it picturable**, and so makes it dual-coded and sticky.

This isn't ornament; it is how minds handle abstraction. **Conceptual metaphor theory** (George Lakoff and Mark Johnson, *Metaphors We Live By*, 1980) showed that we routinely understand an abstract **target domain** through a concrete **source domain** — LOVE IS A JOURNEY, TIME IS MONEY, ARGUMENT IS WAR — and that the mapping runs one direction only: from the concrete and bodily *toward* the abstract (**unidirectionality**). We borrow what we can touch to grasp what we can't.

For the lyricist this is the master move for rendering a feeling: **don't name the abstraction — map it onto a concrete source you can picture,** and let the source's details carry the feeling. *Grief* → a house with every light still on for someone who isn't coming back. *Time running out* → coins thinning in a pocket. Choose a source domain whose built-in logic (its *entailments*) matches the feeling you want, then write the source concretely and let the listener feel the target.

Two connections. A *fresh* metaphor is a fresh **pairing of domains** — yoking two fields that have never met — which is the same freshness move as rhyming distant ideas in `rhymingWords.md`, one layer up. And a **dead metaphor** (a mapping worn smooth — *broken heart*, *burning desire*) has gone invisible: it no longer projects any picture, so it has quietly slid back into abstraction. Revive it or replace it.

## 4. The ladder of abstraction: choosing the rung

Concreteness isn't binary; it's a **ladder**. The semanticist S.I. Hayakawa (*Language in Thought and Action*, 1939) placed every referent on rungs from specific to general: *Bessie* → *cow* → *livestock* → *farm asset* → *wealth*. Each step up sheds detail and picture; each step down adds it.

Good writing travels up *and* down the ladder — but the working rule for lyrics is: **climb down to the lowest rung the line can carry** for anything that must be seen and remembered. *Bessie* pictures; *livestock* doesn't. The **telling detail** — one specific, named, low-rung thing (a chipped blue mug, a particular street, a brand on a bottle) — implies the whole scene and earns belief in a way a general statement never can.

This yields a useful paradox: **the more specific, the more universal.** A precise particular invites the listener to lay their *own* particular over it (the same mechanism as object writing — they fill it with their stuff). Vague, "relatable" language connects with no one, because there's nothing for the listener to grip. Name the one true detail.

When *should* you climb up? For the turn — the line that names what the images have meant. Spend most of the song low and concrete; visit the high, abstract rung deliberately and rarely, and it will ring like a bell because everything around it was solid ground.

## 5. Emotional charge: valence and arousal

Emotionally charged words — positive *or* negative — are remembered better than neutral ones; this is the **emotion-enhanced memory effect.** *Chocolate* and *murder* both out-stick *status*. Two dimensions are at work: **valence** (how positive or negative) and **arousal** (how activating). A large 2024 re-analysis found memory rises at *both* ends of valence (a U-shape — strong feeling either way beats neutral) and climbs steadily with arousal. Arousal flags a word as behaviorally significant, and the brain preferentially banks the significant.

Stack this on §1 and you get the **stickiest unit in the language: a vivid, picturable, emotionally loaded word.** *Blood* out-sticks *injury*; *knife* out-sticks *danger*; *slam* out-sticks *conflict*. Concreteness and charge multiply.

Two cautions. Charge is a spice, not the meal — a line where every word is high-arousal exhausts the ear and cancels out, since nothing stands against anything (the placement discipline of `phoneticAppeal.md` §8 applies to meaning too). And a charged *abstraction* — *agony, ecstasy, despair* — is still telling. Prefer the charged *concrete*: not *agony*, but the clenched thing that causes it.

## 6. Diction register: Germanic vs. Latinate

English carries two vocabularies for almost everything, and the choice between them sets a line's temperature. Short **Germanic / Anglo-Saxon** words (*think, burn, light, fight, bone, cold, break*) versus longer **Latinate** ones (*consider, incinerate, illumination, altercation, skeleton, frigid, fracture*). The old style guides made a rule of it — the Fowler brothers' *The King's English* (1906): *prefer the concrete word to the abstract, prefer the Saxon word to the Romance* — and Orwell, Churchill, Strunk & White, and Ward Farnsworth all echo it.

The difference is exactly the difference this document keeps circling. Germanic words are shorter, harder-edged, more concrete, faster, and **easier to picture**; Latinate words are abstract, softer, more musical, and slower — Farnsworth notes a "friction," a half-beat of translation before a Latinate word reaches the mind's eye. The research agrees: in English, abstract concepts cluster in Latinate vocabulary while concrete nouns skew Germanic.

For lyrics, **Germanic is the default** — concrete, urgent, and singable (monosyllabic, hard-consonant; see `phoneticAppeal.md`). Reach for a Latinate word *on purpose*: for irony, for a cold or clinical or institutional distance, for formality, or simply for the texture of one long soft word set among hard short ones. *"The dissolution of us"* feels very different from *"us, falling apart"* — and the gap between them is a tool.

Two prosodic notes. Germanic monosyllables carry **flexible stress**, so they drop onto any beat without fighting the hit; a Latinate polysyllable locks its stress and risks the *greedy spot* (`prosodyMeter.md`) when the accent lands on the wrong syllable. And their open, hard-edged sounds sit naturally on the hits and the money-note (`phoneticAppeal.md`). The plainer, older word is usually the one that *sings*.

## 7. The verb is the engine

The verb carries the **action** — and the energy — of a line. Bury the action and the line goes inert, no matter how good the nouns are.

The classic way to kill a verb is **nominalization** — turning it into an abstract noun. Helen Sword named these **"zombie nouns"** (2012); they are also called **buried** or **hidden verbs.** *Decide* becomes *decision*, *move* becomes *movement*, *fail* becomes *failure*, usually via a suffix like *-tion, -ment, -ance, -ity, -ing*. Once the action is embalmed in a noun, a limp crutch verb (*is, make, do, have, feel*) has to prop up the line:

> *"I feel the sensation of falling"* → **"I'm falling."**
> *"There was a slow breaking inside me"* → **"something slowly broke."**

The fix is always the same: **return the action to a strong, concrete, active verb, with a concrete subject doing it.** *Shoulder, splinter, slam, drift, ache, rust, buckle, claw* — vivid verbs outperform flat verbs and abstract nouns at once, and they are where the Germanic register (§6) does its best work. The verb is also the most underused site of imagery: writers load adjectives onto a weak verb when a single strong verb would carry the whole picture. (Nominalization has honest uses — summarizing a prior action so a line can move on — but in a lyric the default is the live verb.)

## 8. Frequency vs. defamiliarization: the fluency/surprise dial

A line is pulled two ways at once. **High-frequency, common words** process fastest and feel natural and singable — which is why hooks overwhelmingly use plain words plus direct address (*you*) for intimacy. Ease is liking; fluency is persuasion (the processing-fluency principle, `phoneticAppeal.md` §1).

But *total* familiarity is forgettable. The **isolation effect** (von Restorff) says the one distinctive item in a uniform field is the item remembered. And **defamiliarization** — the Russian Formalist Viktor Shklovsky's *ostranenie*, "making strange" (*Art as Technique*, 1917) — holds that habit deadens perception, and art's job is to make the ordinary strange again so it can be *seen*. The strange word wakes the ear, at the cost of a beat of extra processing; that cost is the point.

The synthesis is the pop ideal: **a fluent, singable frame of common words with one or two specific, surprising, concrete, well-sounded words planted in the key position.** Fluency gets the line liked; the spike of surprise gets it remembered. And *where* the spike goes matters as much as that it exists — put it on the hit, the rhyme, the syllable the whole line builds toward (the placement discipline of `phoneticAppeal.md` §8, aimed at the strong positions: the hits and downbeats). One unexpected word in the right slot, inside an easy frame, is the whole game.

## 9. Stream of consciousness: coherence without narrative

The sections above chose the *word*; this one is about how a *run* of words holds together. Most lyrics cohere on the **meaning** axis — line B follows from line A by story or argument. But coherence can leave that axis entirely. Cut the narrative thread and let the lines hold together by other means, and you get writing that reads less like a *report* of an experience than like the **texture of a mind in motion** — dreamlike, vibe-forward, immersive.

The name is William James's. In *The Principles of Psychology* (1890) he called consciousness a **stream** — not a jointed chain of logical links but a continuous flow that moves by association: in his phrase, it is nothing jointed; it flows. Dreams run the same way, cohering by resemblance, feeling, and juxtaposition rather than plot. A lyric built this way reproduces the *shape of thinking* about something instead of narrating it, which is the peculiar intimacy of the mode. The modernists built it into fiction as **interior monologue** (Joyce, Woolf, Faulkner); in song it descends from the French **Symbolists** — Rimbaud's "derangement of the senses," the program of evoking *indirectly* — through the Beats into Dylan's mid-1960s image-torrents, and into a great deal of the best hip-hop, where bars cohere by rhyme, flow, wordplay, and persona rather than by story.

**The load-bearing idea: coherence relocates.** Continuity doesn't vanish when you cut narrative; it moves onto other axes. Four carry the weight:

- **Sound** — assonance chains, internal rhyme, and rhythm pull you line to line where logic used to (`phoneticAppeal.md`). The sound *is* the connective tissue.
- **Image** — one consistent sensory world: the same light, weather, and register of objects — the object-writing of §2 aimed at building a *world* rather than a *plot*.
- **Mood** — one stable emotional temperature, so every line, however disconnected in sense, vibrates at the same frequency.
- **Voice** — one speaker, attitude, and diction throughout; the *I* holds still while the thoughts leap.

Nothing follows, but everything *belongs*.

**The trade, and the discipline.** The more you loosen meaning, the tighter you must hold those four — it is a trade, not a free pass. This makes dream-logic writing *harder* than narrative, not easier: you have thrown away the strongest rope (story) and must carry the whole weight on sound, image, mood, and voice. "Random words that happen to scan" is what you get when you cut narrative and *don't* pay for it elsewhere — the abstraction-stuffing this document warns against (§12). The version that works is a discipline, not a license.

**The buried center.** The strongest form isn't pure vibe — it is organized around a single **unnamed feeling** that every fragment resonates with but none states. The literary name for this is T.S. Eliot's **objective correlative** (*Hamlet and His Problems*, 1919): a set of objects, a situation, a chain of events that is the *formula* of a particular emotion, so that giving the external facts evokes the feeling without naming it. At the scale of a whole verse this is **show, don't tell** raised one level: you never say the feeling; the accumulated images deposit it in the listener. It also supplies the **selection rule** — the test for the next line is not "does this advance the story?" but "does this image, however unconnected on the surface, belong to the *emotional field* of the hidden center?" The images are visible stars; the feeling is the invisible gravity holding them in orbit, and that gravity is what makes a scatter of fragments cohere as a *vibe* instead of dissolving into noise.

**Juxtaposition is the engine.** The spark comes from collision: set two unrelated images side by side and the listener's mind makes a *third thing* in the gap — meaning generated, not stated. The Surrealists made a principle of it. Pierre Reverdy (1918), quoted into André Breton's *Manifesto of Surrealism* (1924): the image springs from a juxtaposition of two more or less distant realities, and the more distant *and true* the relationship, the stronger the image. It is the same freshness move as a distant rhyme pairing (`rhymingWords.md` §7) or a fresh metaphor domain (§3) — collision across distance — now run as the through-line. And it is deeply participatory: you hand over raw materials and let the listener assemble the feeling, the "they fill it with their own stuff" of §2, kin to the gap you leave in `callResponse.md`.

**The anchor is a lever, not a switch.** Where you place the recurring handhold the leaps return to is a real choice:

- **Global** — a refrain or recurring image the whole song circles: a constellation with one bright central star. Most hypnotic and hook-friendly.
- **Sectional** — unmoored verses (held only by sound, image, mood, and voice) resolving to an **anchored chorus** where the recurring image arrives. This is **section-level call-and-response** (`callResponse.md`): the verse withholds the handhold precisely so the chorus can deliver it, and the verse's groundlessness is what makes the chorus land like relief.
- **None** — held only by the invisible gravity and the sound; most immersive and purely dreamlike, but it must stay oblique, since with no handhold one too-literal line reads as a dead spot.

Crucially, an anchor never has to *name* the feeling: a recurring image or act (*leave the light on*) is a handhold, not a confession. You keep the never-say-it rule even while giving the listener somewhere to land.

**Threading the needle.** Two opposite failures, and the buried feeling is the compass out of both. **Too explicit** — a line names the feeling aloud and the spell breaks, because the whole point was to deliver it sideways. **Too arbitrary** — a line that sounds nice and fits the world but has no tie to the center: filler, a dead spot in the wash. The feeling is what you steer *away from saying* and what you steer every image *toward meaning*.

A sectional anchor in miniature — an unmoored verse resolving to an anchored refrain:

> Frost on the window, the tap running thin,
> a radio drowning in the next room's hymn,
> the coffee goes to skin, the stairs forget they're stairs,
> an engine somewhere warms and leaves —
>
> *so I leave the light on,*
> *so I leave the light on.*

No line in the verse follows from the one before it; it holds by the cold domestic **palette**, the numb **mood**, the steady **voice**, and the sound chains (*thin / hymn*, *stairs / stairs*). The buried feeling — someone gone, or going, and the speaker keeping the small rituals up instead of saying so — is never named. The chorus delivers the one recurring **image-anchor** (the response to the verse's open call), a handhold that still refuses to name the fear.

## 10. Worked example — before / after

> **Before:** *"The sadness I feel is too real to conceal."*

What's wrong, lever by lever:

- **Abstraction (§1).** *Sadness* is a concept, not an image — single-coded, low-imageability, lands soft.
- **Telling, not showing (§1–2).** It *names* the feeling instead of building a scene the listener can enter.
- **Top of the ladder (§4).** Every noun sits on a high, general rung; there is no detail to grip, so nothing is universal.
- **Dead verb (§7).** *Feel* is a crutch verb; the real action is buried.
- **Over-resolved rhyme (`rhymingWords.md`).** *Feel / real / conceal* is a three-way perfect rhyme — sing-songy, more closure than an open wound deserves.

> **After:** *"The kettle's gone cold and the door's still open wide."*

Why it lands:

- **Concrete, picturable, low-rung images (§1, §4)** — *kettle, cold, door* — let the listener *infer* the loneliness and refill it with their own.
- **A live verb and a telling detail (§7, §4)** — the kettle *gone cold*, the door left *open* — carry the whole story of a sudden absence without naming it.
- **Germanic, singable diction (§6)** — every word is short, hard-edged, and easy to sing.
- **Unresolved on purpose** — it ends open, on the long open vowel of *wide* with no rhyme yet, leaving the feeling hanging, which matches a lingering sadness (the stable/unstable lens, `prosodyMeter.md`).
- **The emotion is stronger precisely because it is never named.**

A second, smaller fix shows the metaphor move (§3): *"I was overwhelmed by anxiety"* → *"the walls leaned in."* The abstraction (*anxiety*) is mapped onto a concrete, kinesthetic source (rooms closing) — now it pictures, and the dread is felt rather than reported.

## 11. Checklist

- [ ] Are the key words **concrete and picturable** (could you photograph them), or are you stuffing abstractions?
- [ ] Are you **showing** the emotion through an image and letting the listener infer it, rather than **naming** it?
- [ ] If the subject is abstract, have you **mapped it onto a concrete source** (metaphor) instead of stating it?
- [ ] Have you **climbed down to a telling detail** — one specific, named, low-rung thing — for the images that must stick?
- [ ] Is the **verb** carrying the action, or is it buried in a **zombie noun** propped up by *is / make / feel*?
- [ ] Is the **register** right — Germanic by default for punch and singability, Latinate only where you *want* distance or irony?
- [ ] One **surprising, specific, well-sounded** word planted in the **key position**, inside an otherwise fluent, singable frame?
- [ ] Are the most **emotionally charged** words also the most **concrete** — and used sparingly enough to register?
- [ ] If you've **loosened the narrative thread**, is the lyric still held — by sound, image, mood, and voice — with every fragment resonating with one **buried feeling** you never name?

## 12. Common failures

**Abstract-noun stuffing.** Lines built on *pain, struggle, journey, freedom, soul.* Low imageability, soft texture, forgettable. *Fix:* replace with concrete, often monosyllabic, sensory nouns, and let the image imply the feeling.

**Zombie nouns.** The action smothered into *-tion / -ment* nouns, leaving weak crutch verbs to do the work. *Fix:* dig the verb back out — *I'm falling*, not *I feel the sensation of falling*.

**Naming the emotion.** *Telling* the feeling (*so sad, such joy, the pain inside*) instead of staging the scene that causes it. *Fix:* cut the emotion-word; write the kettle.

**Vague mistaken for universal.** Reaching for general, "relatable" language to include everyone, which grips no one. *Fix:* the more specific the detail, the more universal the reach — name the one true thing.

**Dead metaphor.** *Broken heart, burning love, weight of the world* — worn so smooth they project no picture. *Fix:* a fresh pairing of domains, or a literal image instead.

**Latinate fog.** Long, soft, abstract words where short, hard, concrete ones were available — distance and friction you didn't intend. *Fix:* prefer the Saxon word, unless the distance is the point.

**All spike, no frame.** Every word surprising or high-arousal, so nothing stands out and the ear fatigues. *Fix:* one or two spikes in the key positions; trust the plain words around them.

**Untethered drift.** Associative writing that coheres on *nothing* — not story, not sound/image/mood/voice, not a buried center — so it reads as random. *Fix:* cut narrative only if you pay for it on the other axes, and steer every fragment toward one unnamed feeling (§9).

**Saying the secret.** In a vibe-forward, dream-logic passage, a line that names the buried feeling outright and breaks the spell. *Fix:* deliver it sideways through the images; never state it.

## 13. Glossary

- **Concreteness / imageability** — how picturable a word is; high-imageability words are remembered far better (dual coding).
- **Dual coding** — Paivio's theory that imageable words are stored as both language and mental image, giving two retrieval routes.
- **Concreteness effect** — concrete words are recalled roughly twice as well as matched abstract words; among the most replicated memory findings.
- **Picture-superiority effect** — actual images are remembered better than words; the limit case of dual coding.
- **Object writing** — Pattison's sense-bound free-association exercise for generating concrete material.
- **Seven senses** — sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, plus **organic** (inner-body awareness) and **kinesthetic** (motion and the body in space).
- **Conceptual metaphor** — Lakoff & Johnson's account of understanding an abstract **target domain** through a concrete **source domain**; the mapping runs concrete → abstract.
- **Dead metaphor** — a metaphor worn so familiar it no longer projects an image.
- **Ladder of abstraction** — Hayakawa's scale from specific to general (*Bessie → cow → livestock → wealth*); climb down for picturable detail.
- **Telling detail** — one specific, low-rung particular that implies a whole scene and earns belief.
- **Valence / arousal** — a word's positivity/negativity and its activating intensity; both raise memorability (the emotion-enhanced memory effect).
- **Germanic / Latinate** — English's two vocabularies: short, concrete, singable Anglo-Saxon words vs. longer, abstract, slower Latin-derived ones.
- **Nominalization (zombie noun / buried verb)** — a verb turned into an abstract noun (*decide → decision*), draining a line's action and forcing weak crutch verbs.
- **Defamiliarization (ostranenie)** — Shklovsky's "making strange": renewing perception of the ordinary by presenting it in an unfamiliar way.
- **Isolation effect (von Restorff)** — the one distinctive item in a uniform field is the best remembered.
- **Stream of consciousness** — writing that mimics the associative flow of thought (James, 1890); with the narrative thread dropped, coherence is carried by sound, image, mood, and voice.
- **Objective correlative** — Eliot's term for a set of images or events that is the *formula* of an emotion, evoking it without naming it; the buried-center technique.
- **Juxtaposition (the surrealist image)** — meaning struck from the collision of two distant images (Reverdy/Breton); the engine of associative writing.
- **Processing fluency** — the ease of taking something in; experienced as liking and trust (see `phoneticAppeal.md`).

---

*These are the dials of vivid diction. Which way a given writer turns them — how concrete, how charged, how strange, how Germanic, how dense, how associative — is not a craft question but a voice question. That is what `voiceStyle.md` is for.*
