# lineCombo

The lineCombo (or just **combo**) is the atomic creative unit of lyricist. This document covers what a combo is, the aesthetic it serves, how to build a good one, and what separates a combo that lands from one that falls flat. It owns the combo as *craft*; how a combo is written down lives in `calileiNotation.md`. Examples here are plain text with analytical labels.

> Labels used below: **L1 / L2** = line 1 / line 2. **[A] / [B]** = the first and second half (linePart) of a line. These are analytical labels, not the written notation.

## 1. What a combo is

Three nested units, smallest to largest:

- **line** — one line of lyric. A combo is exactly **two** lines.
- **linePart** — half of a line. A line can split into **two** lineParts (a first half [A] and a second half [B]) divided by a caesura — a beat of pause, usually heard as a comma. A line can also be a single, undivided part.
- **lineCombo** — the full two-line unit.

The defining quality of a combo is **call and response nested inside call and response**:

- The two **lines** answer each other (this is the *macro* call-and-response).
- Inside each line, the two **halves** answer each other (this is the *micro* call-and-response).

So a fully-built combo is four moving parts arranged as a call that gets answered, twice over, at two scales at once:

```
L1:  [A] calls  →  [B] answers
            ↓  (L1 calls)
L2:  [A] calls  →  [B] answers   (L2 answers L1)
```

That nesting is the whole identity of the form. A couplet that merely rhymes is not a combo. A combo is a couplet where the second line *responds* to the first, and ideally where each line's second half responds to its own first half.

### Why exactly two

The two-line count is not a convention to relax — it is load-bearing, for three reasons:

- **The two-ness *is* the concept.** A combo is call→response. At one line there is no response; at three, the pairwise relationship breaks down — line 3 has to answer *which* line? The definition only stays crisp at two.
- **It keeps the generation target constant.** The whole writing framework optimizes combo by combo. If a combo is always two lines, Claude always knows exactly what it is writing, regardless of how long the section is.
- **Everything bigger composes from it.** A four-line block is two combos; a verse is a run of combos (with the occasional tag). Fixing the unit at two is what lets sections of any length build cleanly out of the same repeating piece (§11).

## 2. Completeness over continuity — the aesthetic

This section is the *why* behind every rule that follows. The style runs on **closure, not plot.**

A normal verse holds your attention by making you wonder what happens next; it pays off at the end of a story. This style refuses that bargain. It holds attention by making **each combo land** — open a small loop, snap it shut — so the pleasure comes from a steady *rhythm of resolutions* rather than a single arc. You don't get one big payoff at the end; you get a clean little one every two lines.

That reverses the usual priorities:

- **Continuity is deprioritized.** Combos don't need to connect, advance a plot, or share a subject. You are not telling a story.
- **Completeness is prioritized.** Each combo must be a *complete thought* — a statement, question, image, or claim that finishes inside its own two lines. The listener isn't following a thread; they're catching a series of fully-formed thoughts.

This is the most important principle for *creating* good combos, because it changes where effort goes: every combo gets the full setup-and-payoff treatment instead of leaning on context the listener may not have.

### The landing

Every combo has to give the ear a place to **rest** — a point where the *sound* (the end-rhyme) and the *sense* (the finished thought) click shut at the same instant, almost always at the end of L2. That click is the **landing**, and it is effectively the listener's breath.

This is the line between the style and its failure. With landings, the listener's mind gets to set down on something solid, combo after combo. Without them — pure floating images that never resolve — the verse goes **"too dream-like": attention keeps falling forward and never gets to rest on anything.** The combo is the device that prevents that. Protect the landing above almost everything else.

### What holds a verse together instead of plot

If not narrative, what keeps a verse from feeling random? Four binders, none of them a story:

- **Voice** — one speaker, one attitude, start to finish.
- **World** — combos draw from a shared imagery-bank (the block, money, loyalty, the come-up, the opps).
- **Tone** — the emotional weather stays constant even as the topic jumps.
- **Sound** — flow, cadence, and a consistent density of slant rhyme bind by ear.

A verse coheres as **one mood or persona shown from many angles** — not as a sequence of events.

### The mosaic

So picture a verse as a **mosaic** (or a constellation): each combo is a discrete tile, and the picture emerges from the tiles *sitting together*, not from lines drawn between them. You're rendering a single vibe from many facets, in almost any order. This sits deliberately in the middle of a spectrum:

```
pure narrative  ←——  discrete complete combos  ——→  pure dream-logic
(every line                 (this style)             (nothing resolves,
 advances a plot)                                     everything floats)
```

The combo keeps you off the dream-logic end — each tile resolves. The no-narrative stance keeps you off the plot end. Aim for the middle on purpose.

## 3. Kinds of complete thought

A combo can be "complete" in several different ways. Naming the kind helps you finish the thought cleanly — and, across a verse, **vary the kind from combo to combo** so the mosaic has texture instead of flattening into a list.

- **Statement** — a declaration that resolves.
  > *Made peace with the nights I don't sleep, / some debts you just carry, don't keep.*
- **Question → answer** — asked and answered inside the combo.
  > *They ask did the money change me? / Yeah — into someone who won't explain me.*
- **Image** — a picture painted until it snaps into focus.
  > *Smoke in the kitchen, blue light on the wall, / phone face-down so it can't call.*
- **Observation** — a noticing about how things are that lands as true.
  > *Funny how loyalty's loud till it costs, / then everybody quiet, countin' they loss.*
- **Boast / flex** — a claim plus its proof or intensifier.
  > *Came with nothin', built the whole lane, / now they study the route I came.*
- **Scene** — a tiny moment set up and closed; narrative, but kept *local* to the one combo.
  > *She left the keys, didn't slam the door, / I knew right then what the silence was for.*
- **Turn / realization** — a setup that flips on L2.
  > *Thought the come-up would feel like a win, / turned out it just let the real ones in.*
- **Exchange** — two voices, call-and-response as actual dialogue.
  > *"You good?" — "I'm always good," / never the truth, but it's understood.*

The macro moves in §4 are *how* a combo answers itself; these kinds are *what sort of complete thing* it is. A strong verse mixes both freely, and a run of eight identical kinds (all flexes, say) is the fastest way to wallpaper.

## 4. The two axes of call and response

A strong combo works on both axes. Treat the lists below as a **vocabulary of moves**, not a checklist — they name the kinds of relationships that create a satisfying answer.

### Macro axis — L1 calls, L2 responds

How does L2 *answer* L1? Common moves:

- **Setup → payoff.** L1 plants a premise, scene, or question; L2 cashes it.
  > *Walked in expecting nothing at all, / Left with my name on the wall.*
- **Then → now.** L1 is the past, L2 is the present. Contrast across time.
  > *Counted singles just to eat, / Now I lose more than I keep.*
- **Claim → consequence.** L1 is an action or assertion; L2 is what follows from it.
  > *I stopped explaining myself, / Funny how that cleared the shelf.*
- **Concession → rebuttal.** L1 grants something; L2 pivots on a "but."
  > *Yeah, I made my share of mistakes, / But none of 'em yours to take.*
- **Action → reaction.** L1 is what the speaker does; L2 is how others respond.
  > *I went quiet for a season, / Half of 'em invented a reason.*
- **Two facets / parallel portrait.** Both lines describe one subject from different angles; the response is the second facet completing the picture.
  > *My brother'll start it without a word, / Take the charge so the rest stay heard.*
- **Statement → twist.** L2 recontextualizes L1, often darkly or comically — the knife or the joke lands second.
  > *Everybody wanted in the room, / Nobody wanted to hold the broom.*
- **Boast → reframe.** L2 redefines the terms of L1's claim.
  > *Call it a winning streak, / I call it just bein' me.*

### Micro axis — [A] calls, [B] responds (inside one line)

Within a single line, the first half sets up and the second half pays off. Common moves:

- **Subject → predicate punch.** Short subject up front, strongest word at the very end. The workhorse.
  > *The doubters [A] / got loud after the fact [B].*
- **Action → specification.** Name the action, then sharpen or escalate it.
  > *He moves quiet [A] / aims for the throat [B].*
- **Condition → result.** "If X" then "Y."
  > *If it's silent [A] / then it's already done [B].*
- **Question → answer.** Rhetorical question, immediate answer.
  > *Need a way out? [A] / I know a door [B].*
- **Claim → intensifier.** Assert, then push it further.
  > *I want the whole thing [A] / nothin' less [B].*
- **Image → turn.** Concrete image, then a flip on it.
  > *Built it from dirt [A] / won't let it wash [B].*

## 5. How the axes interact

The best combos pull on **both** axes, but they are not equally essential:

- **The macro response is mandatory.** If L2 does not answer L1 in *some* way, you have two unrelated lines, not a combo — no matter how clever each line is on its own. This is the most common thing that gets missed.
- **The micro response is the multiplier.** Splitting each line into a sharp [A]→[B] adds density and a second satisfying click, but a combo of two *undivided* lines that answer each other cleanly is still a real, working combo.

Three healthy configurations:

1. **Full four-part** — both lines split. Maximum density and parallelism; the most "locked-in" feel. Use when you want symmetry and momentum.
2. **One split, one undivided** — one line breathes while the other punches in two beats. The most natural-sounding mix.
3. **A pause occupies one half** — silence stands in for a linePart, and the remaining words land harder for the empty beat around them.

Reaching for the full four-part shape *every* time is a trap — it reads as mechanical. Vary the configuration combo to combo (see §7).

## 6. Rhyme architecture

- **The defining rhyme is the pair of hits — one per line.** The binding rhyme lands on each line's **hit** (the accented beat the ear leans on), not necessarily on its last word. Usually the hit falls at or near the end of the line, so it reads as an end-rhyme — but the load-bearing match is on the *accent*, and it is sometimes **multisyllabic**, anchored on the hit and running forward into the next word or two as bonus ear candy. This pair of hit-rhymes is what binds the two lines into one unit — and it is half of the landing (§2): the sound clicking shut at the same moment the sense does. (The mechanics of hit-anchored rhyme live in `rhymingWords.md` §6; where the hit falls *in time* — against the instrumental pulse, and how it bounces — is `comboBounce.md`.)
- **Favor slant and multisyllabic rhymes over strict ones.** Rhyme on the *sound*, not the spelling. Slant pairs (real / feel, strange / same, important / portrait) sound more modern and give you far more room to keep the meaning you actually want.
- **Internal rhyme is a bonus.** Echoes between [A] and [B], or carried across the two lines, add texture — but they are seasoning, not the structure.
- **Never bend the meaning to force the rhyme.** If a clean image and a perfect rhyme fight, keep the image and take the slant rhyme. The end of the line is also where the strongest *meaning* should sit, so a rhyme that forces a weak or nonsense word there does double damage.

## 7. Variation — when the shape flexes

A verse of nothing but symmetrical four-part combos feels like a machine. The variations below are what make it sound human; reach for them on purpose.

- **Pause in [B].** The first half lands, then silence. The empty beat lets the line harden before the next line comes.
  > *I said what I said, (beat) / read it back, it still ain't dead.*
- **Pause in [A].** The line front-loads the silence and pushes its payoff late.
  > *Told 'em keep my name out they mouth, / (beat) now they figurin' it out.*
- **Undivided line.** One continuous thought, no internal split, no micro response. Often best as L1, setting up a two-part L2.
  > *The block remembers every face that came through, / some stayed down, most of 'em flew.*
- **Asymmetry between the lines.** L1 and L2 do not have to share a shape. A long undivided L1 answered by a tight, split L2 creates a satisfying tightening.

(A pause as a half is a **silent linePart** — one half carries words, the other is rests. `calileiNotation.md` writes it directly.)

## 8. How to build a good combo

There is no single order; pick whichever entry point gives you traction, then build outward.

**Entry points:**

1. **Start from the payoff.** Decide the single strongest line or image first — usually whatever ends L2 — then build *backward*: what setup makes that hit hardest?
2. **Start from the rhyme pair.** Choose two end-sounds that rhyme (slant is fine) *and* can both carry real meaning, then write toward them.
3. **Start from the relationship.** Pick a macro move from §4 (then→now, concession→rebuttal, statement→twist) and fill the slots to fit it.
4. **Start from one strong image** and hunt for its response — the line that answers, twists, or completes it.

**Then, for the single combo:**

- **Put the strongest word at the end** of each line, on the rhyme.
- **Confirm L2 actually responds to L1.** Say it plainly to yourself: "L2 answers L1 by ______." If you can't fill that blank, it isn't a combo yet.
- **Protect the landing.** Make sure sound and sense resolve together at the end of L2 (§2). If the rhyme lands but the thought doesn't finish, the combo leaks.
- **Split the lines if it earns a second click** — add a micro [A]→[B] move where it sharpens, but don't force a split that adds filler.
- **Cut every part to its load-bearing words.** No padding syllables propping up the meter.
- **Read it aloud** for cadence; the caesura and the line-ends should fall where the beats want them.

**Then, across the verse:**

- **Apply the reorder test.** A finished combo survives being moved. Shuffle the combos in a verse: if it still reads fine, they're properly self-contained. If shuffling breaks one, it was leaning on its neighbor — that's smuggled-in narrative; cut the dependency.
- **Vary the kind (§3) and the configuration (§5).** Don't let consecutive combos share the same kind of completeness *and* the same shape — that's how a mosaic flattens into a list.
- **Sequence by feel, not logic.** Since plot isn't ordering them, arrange combos for *dynamics* — contrast, build, a peak, a cool-down — and for sound. You're arranging tiles for the eye, not events on a timeline.
- **Bind, don't connect.** Hold the verse together with voice, world, and tone (§2), not with a thread running between combos.

## 9. Failure modes

### Per-combo (visible in a single combo, read alone)

**Restatement instead of response.** L2 just rephrases L1; nothing gets answered.
> ✗ *I got money now, I'm doin' good, / I got cash today like I knew I would.*
> ✓ *Money came quick, I won't pretend, / the cash made strangers, lost a friend.*
> *(Now L2 turns on L1: the cost of the come-up, not a second helping of it.)*

**Forced rhyme.** The meaning bends to hit the sound.
> ✗ *I came up from the bottom floor, / now I purchase oranges at the store.*
> ✓ *I came up from the bottom floor, / same hunger, just don't show it no more.*
> *(Keep the come-up; take the slant rhyme; the meaning survives.)*

**Filler.** Padding syllables hold up the meter.
> ✗ *And I was really just sittin' there thinkin' 'bout how / I could maybe possibly get it right now.*
> ✓ *Sat there plottin', didn't make a sound, / next thing they know, I'm holdin' it down.*

**Buried payoff.** The strongest word sits in the middle, not the end.
> ✗ *They never believed the vision I had inside, / now the same people copy my stride proudly.*
> ✓ *They never believed the vision was real, / now they bite the blueprint, copy the feel.*
> *(Strong word — "feel" / "real" — moved to the line-end, on the rhyme.)*

**Register drift.** The voice or attitude changes mid-combo. Hold one speaker, one register, both lines.

### Whole-verse (only visible across combos)

- **Too dream-like.** Combos don't land; pure images with no resolution; attention never rests. *Fix:* give every combo a clean point of rest — sound and sense resolving together at the end of L2 (§2).
- **List-like / monotony.** Every combo is the same *kind* (all flexes) in the same shape and cadence; the mosaic flattens to wallpaper. *Fix:* vary the kind (§3) and the configuration (§5).
- **Narrative dependency.** A combo only makes sense after the one before it. *Fix:* make it self-contained; apply the reorder test (§8).
- **Vibe drift.** Combos share no world or tone, so the mosaic forms no coherent picture. *Fix:* bind by voice, imagery, and emotional weather (§2).

## 10. Quality checklist

A combo is done when:

- [ ] **L2 demonstrably responds to L1** — you can name the macro move.
- [ ] **It lands** — sound and sense resolve together at L2's end, giving the ear a place to rest.
- [ ] The **end-rhyme pair** binds the two lines (slant is fine).
- [ ] The **strongest word/image lands at each line-end**.
- [ ] Every linePart is **tight** — no filler.
- [ ] The whole thing holds **one voice, one register**.
- [ ] It **survives being read alone**, and **survives the reorder test** — no dependence on its neighbors.
- [ ] It's a **different kind of complete thought** (§3) than the combos around it.
- [ ] It **shares the verse's voice / world / tone** even though it shares no plot.
- [ ] If it splits into halves, each **[A]→[B]** also earns its own small response.

## 11. Trails and larger structures

A combo is **always exactly two lines** — the call and the response. Everything else is built *around* a combo, not folded into it. Holding this line firmly is what keeps the unit (and the generation target) crisp.

### Trails

A **trail** is a standalone line that lands *after* a combo to cap, resolve, or punctuate it — a button, not a response. It sits **outside** the combo's two-line call-and-response: it doesn't answer a call, it lands a final hit once the call-and-response is already complete.

- A trail has no mark of its own: it is simply a standalone line placed **after** the combo, sitting **outside** the combo unit, and its **position** binds it to the combo it follows. Because it sits outside the combo, it has no combo to anchor its rhyme — so if the trail should rhyme, its rhyme is marked by hand. (`calileiNotation.md` writes a trail exactly this way: a line outside the combo, rhyme tagged by hand.)
- A trail is usually short and undivided, but it's still a full line — it may split into halves if that sharpens the punch.
- More than one trail can follow a combo; each line binds back to the same combo.

Use a trail when the pair has said its piece and you want one more beat to drive it home — not to smuggle in a third line of call-and-response that the combo couldn't hold.

> *Example shape:* a mirrored couplet (the combo) followed by a tight one-liner that nails the point shut (the trail).

### Larger blocks compose from combos

Don't reach for a "three-line combo" or "four-line combo." A four-line block is **two combos** back to back — call→response, then call→response. Keeping the combo fixed at two lines is exactly what lets sections of any length build cleanly out of the same repeating unit.

(One genuine exception exists — the interleaved quatrain, where line 3 answers line 1 and line 4 answers line 2, with the rhymes crossing the pair boundary. That is not two combos, but it's rare in this style and gets its own treatment if and when a real example calls for it.)

## How this doc sits in the framework

The combo is the **default creative unit** for this voice — when filling a spec, build combo by combo unless the structure says otherwise. This doc owns the combo as craft; the rest of the stack connects to it:

- `calileiNotation.md` — how a combo is written down: the `{combo}` / `[line]` / `(linePart)` units, the hits that anchor the rhyme, the caesura and the silent half, and the trail as a line outside the combo. This doc is the craft; that one is the notation.
- `comboBounce.md` — the **timing half** of the same unit: how the combo places against the instrumental pulse (the anchor), where its hits fall in time, and the bounce that comes off that offset. This doc owns the combo's *meaning and rhyme*; that one owns its *cadence*. Siblings.
- `callResponse.md` — the general call-and-response device the combo is built from (the gap, antiphony, periods, sections); the combo is its signature nesting.
- `rhymingWords.md` §6 — the hit-anchored, multisyllabic rhyme that binds the two lines.
