# Phonetic Appeal — Designing How a Line Sounds

*Making lyrics that sound good to hear and feel good to say.*

Most advice about lyrics focuses on **writing** — what to say, what story to tell, what emotion to land. This document is about something else: **lyric design**, the craft of arranging sound itself so that a line is satisfying to deliver and pleasurable to hear, largely independent of what it means.

A well-written line communicates. A well-designed line *physically rewards* the mouth that speaks it and the ear that hears it. The best lyrics do both — and the two reinforce each other: a line that is easy and pleasing to produce is also easier to perceive, remember, and believe. But they are not the same skill, and design is the one that usually goes unnamed and unpracticed.

Phonetic appeal rests on two pillars: **the mechanics of sound** (what the mouth is doing, and which sequences feel natural versus jagged) and **the placement of sonic devices** (alliteration, assonance, repetition — used as deliberately structured ornament, not decoration sprinkled everywhere). This document makes both explicit, gives you vocabulary for diagnosing why a line works, and supplies checklists to run a draft through.

> Rhyme is a sonic device too — it is the syllable-level layer — but it is rich enough to live in its own file. This document covers the sound layers *around* rhyme; see `rhymingWords.md` for rhyme itself, and `prosodyMeter.md` for matching stress to the beat.

## 1. Processing fluency: the one idea underneath everything

If you remember one concept, make it this: **processing fluency** — the ease with which a mind takes something in. It is the hidden variable behind nearly every "feels good" judgment in this document.

The finding from cognitive science is that the features which make a sound sequence **easy to produce** (in the mouth) overlap heavily with the features that make it **easy to perceive and remember** (in the ear and mind) — and that ease of processing is *itself experienced as pleasure, liking, and even truth*. So "feels nice to say," "sounds good to hear," and "rings true" are not three separate axes. They are mostly **one axis** seen from three angles.

The sharpest evidence is the **rhyme-as-reason effect**: when people rate aphorisms identical in meaning, they judge the **rhyming version as more accurate and truthful**. The rhyme adds fluency, and the mind misattributes that fluency to "this must be right." Repetition produces the same illusion of truth through sheer familiarity.

The practical upshots:

- **Fluency is persuasion.** A well-designed line doesn't just sound nicer; it makes the listener *trust* the sentiment more. This is why slogans, proverbs, and hooks lean so hard on sound.
- **Ease and beauty are the same lever.** When you smooth a line's articulation or tighten its sound-repetition, you are simultaneously making it more memorable and more believable.
- **Familiarity breeds liking.** The **mere-exposure effect** means repeated material becomes more pleasant simply by recurring — the engine under choruses and hooks (see §7).

Hold one caveat alongside this: fluency has a ceiling. Past a certain point, *too* fluent becomes bland and forgettable. The art is a fluent, singable frame with one or two deliberately *striking* elements that snag attention. **Fluency gets you liked; a spike of surprise gets you remembered.**

## 2. The mechanics of sound (articulation)

Every consonant is defined by three things: **where** the sound is made (place of articulation), **how** the airflow is obstructed (manner), and whether the vocal cords vibrate (voicing). These tell you why some word sequences feel like dancing and others like tripping.

### 2.1 Place of articulation

The mouth has a series of "stations" running front (lips) to back (throat):

| Place         | Where                                            | English Examples   |
|  ---          |  ---                                             |  ---               |
| Bilabial      | Both lips                                        | P, B, M            |
| Labiodental   | Lower lip + upper teeth                          | F, V               |
| Dental        | Tongue tip + upper teeth                         | TH (*think, this*) |
| Alveolar      | Tongue tip + ridge behind teeth                  | T, D, S, Z, N, L   |
| Post-alveolar | Tongue + just behind the ridge                   | SH, ZH, CH, J      |
| Palatal       | Tongue body + hard palate                        | Y                  |
| Velar         | Back of tongue + soft palate                     | K, G, NG           |
| Labio-velar   | Lips round *and* tongue body raises at the velum | W                  |
| Glottal       | Vocal folds                                      | H                  |

**Why it matters:** when consecutive consonants are at the same or adjacent stations, the mouth makes a small, cheap movement. When they jump across the mouth, the active articulator travels further, which costs effort and time — read as either a stumble or a deliberate emphasis. Both are tools; you just have to know which one you are deploying. `K → T` (velar → alveolar) is a small frontward hop; `K → P` (velar → bilabial) is a bigger back-to-front leap. Say *kit* then *kip* slowly and you can feel the second closing gesture travel further.

### 2.2 Manner of articulation

How the airflow is shaped:

- **Stops / plosives** (P, B, T, D, K, G) — air fully blocked, then released in a burst. Your **percussion**. A stop is a tiny silence followed by an attack.
- **Fricatives** (F, V, S, Z, SH, ZH, TH, H) — air squeezed through a narrow gap, a sustained hiss. **Texture.** Sibilants (S, SH, Z) cut and hiss; F, V, H whisper.
- **Affricates** (CH, J) — a stop released into a fricative, in one motion. Sharp and rich.
- **Nasals** (M, N, NG) — air resonates through the nose. These **hum and sustain**.
- **Liquids** (L, R) — partial obstruction, voiced, sustainable. These **flow and link**.
- **Glides / approximants** (W, Y) — barely obstructed. Smooth ramps into vowels.

The most useful way to hear this is by **role in the bar**:

| Role                    | Sounds                  | Use on…                                                  |
|---                      |---                      |---                                                       |
| **Percussion / attack** | stops, affricates       | downbeats, accents, the snap at the end of a punchy line |
| **Sustain / legato**    | vowels, nasals, liquids | held notes, melisma, the connective tissue between hits  |
| **Texture / air**       | fricatives, sibilants   | color, hiss, breathiness, menace — used sparingly        |

A line of only fricatives sounds whispered; a line of only stops sounds machine-gunned. The mix is the music.

These three roles map straight onto positions in the bar: percussion sounds want the **accented hits** and the downbeat; sustain sounds want the **held notes** and any melisma; texture is the spice you thread between them. Decide which moment is which, then place the right kind of sound on it.

### 2.3 Voicing

A voiced consonant uses vocal-fold vibration (B, D, G, V, Z, J, M, N, L, R, W, Y); a voiceless one does not (P, T, K, F, S, SH, CH, H, TH-as-in-*think*). Voiceless sounds are **sharper, brighter, quicker**; voiced sounds are **warmer, rounder, slower**.

Most stops and many fricatives come in voiced/voiceless pairs at the same place: P/B, T/D, K/G, F/V, S/Z. You can preserve the *mouth shape* of a line while swapping voicing to change its temperature. *Pat* and *bad* share an articulatory feel; *pat* is harder and brighter, *bad* rounder and warmer. (Voicing lowers a sound's spectral energy, which reads as larger, darker, softer — see the frequency code in §4.)

### 2.4 Vowels

Vowels carry the **pitch, the melody, and most of the acoustic energy** of a line. They are described by tongue and lip posture:

- **High / Low** — how close the tongue is to the roof (*beat* /i/ is high; *bat* /æ/ is low).
- **Front / Back** — tongue forward or pulled back (*see* /i/ front; *sue* /u/ back).
- **Rounded / Unrounded** — whether the lips purse. In English, back vowels (*oo, oh, aw*) are rounded, front vowels (*ee, ay, eh*) unrounded.

Short vowels (*ɪ, ɛ, æ, ʌ, ʊ*) are snappy; long vowels and diphthongs (*ee, oo, eye, oh, ay, ow*) sustain. Front vowels feel **brighter, smaller, sharper**; back/low vowels feel **darker, bigger, more open**.

**Why it matters:** vowel choice is the closest thing you have to writing melody with the mouth. A line that returns to one vowel across its stressed positions feels **anchored**; one that swings widely between vowel positions feels like **motion**. And because sustained pitch lives on vowels, vowel choice is the single biggest factor in how a line *sings* (see §5).

### 2.5 The articulatory pathway

Reading a lyric, the mouth executes an unbroken sequence of postures. Three checks diagnose flow:

1. **Articulatory distance and overlap.** The real cost isn't *direction* — it is *how far the articulators travel* and *whether neighboring gestures can overlap*. Adjacent or compatible sounds can be **co-articulated** (started before the previous one finishes), which is what makes a sequence glide. Large place jumps can't overlap, so they cost time and effort. A consistent sweep tends to feel smooth because it minimizes big back-and-forth excursions, not because the mouth has a preferred compass direction.
2. **Cluster sonority.** Within a syllable, the easiest onsets *rise* in sonority toward the vowel: stop → liquid → vowel (CL, PL, BL, GL, FL, SL, TR, DR, PR, CR, GR…). Clusters that don't rise smoothly (KT, PT) are harder. (Full treatment in §3.)
3. **Cross-syllable junctions.** Even if each syllable is fine, the *seam* matters. Ending one syllable on a stop and starting the next on another stop (*act-ed*) forces a full airflow reset; ending on a liquid or vowel and starting on a vowel glides straight through.

## 3. Sequencing for flow vs. friction

A handful of patterns recur in lines performers call "buttery" — and a handful create the opposite, which you can use on purpose.

### 3.1 The sonority hierarchy

**Sonority** is roughly the inherent loudness/openness of a sound. From most to least:

> **vowels > glides (W, Y) > liquids (L, R) > nasals (M, N, NG) > fricatives > stops**
> *(within obstruents, voiced sounds are slightly more sonorous than their voiceless twins.)*

This single ranking governs how syllables are shaped and which clusters feel easy.

### 3.2 The sonority sequencing principle (the law of the syllable)

The near-universal pattern: **sonority rises into the vowel, then falls after it.** The vowel is the loudness peak; everything ramps up to it and back down.

*plant* → **p**(stop, low) **l**(liquid, higher) **a**(vowel, peak) **n**(nasal) **t**(stop). A clean rise and fall.

A made-up onset like *tlap* is impossible in English because **tl** doesn't rise smoothly. Prefer onsets that respect the rise. **Stop + liquid** (CL, BL, FL, SL, PL, GL, TR, DR, PR, CR, GR…) is the workhorse cluster of English and feels effortless.

**The famous exception:** **S + stop** (SP, ST, SK, and SPR/STR/SKR). These technically violate the rise — /s/ is more sonorous than the following stop — yet feel completely fluent. Linguists treat the /s/ as an "appendix" outside the normal rules. Practically: trust SP/ST/SK.

### 3.3 CV alternation and the babble default

The cleanest possible speech is **consonant–vowel–consonant–vowel**: the mouth alternates closing and opening, with no traffic jam. This is the lowest-effort rhythm the mouth makes — it falls out of the jaw's natural cycle, which is why it dominates the babbling of every baby and why first words across languages are CV reduplications: **mama, papa, dada, baba**.

Two consequences:

- **Reduplication is the most singable shape there is.** *na-na-na, la-la-la, oh-oh, doo-doo, hey-hey* — pure CV repetition, maximally easy to produce, language-independent, minimal in semantic load. This is *why* the most viral hooks are built from vocables.
- **Dense consonant clusters cut against it.** Runs like CCCVCC (*strengths, twelfths*) crowd the mouth. A crowded mouth is a *tense* mouth — useful for effort, grit, or agitation, but it slows delivery. Choose it; don't stumble into it.

### 3.4 Repeating mouth shapes

Lines feel cohesive when consecutive stressed positions repeat *mouth-shape information* — same place, same manner, or same vowel — even when the exact sounds differ. The mouth keeps doing **similar work**, which the ear reads as unity.

This is the key insight under *all* the named sonic devices in §6: alliteration, assonance, consonance, and rhyme are simply **different granularities of getting the mouth to repeat itself.** Naming them separately is useful for placement; underneath, they are one phenomenon.

### 3.5 The arc

Many memorable short hooks trace a deliberate **sonic arc**: begin at one place in the mouth, swing out through an open vowel, and land at a contrasting place. A three-syllable phrase that goes **back → open → front** (velar onset, low open vowel, bilabial or front close) is a complete little physical journey — and a mouth that has just completed a satisfying journey *wants to repeat it*, which is half of what "quotable" means. Treat the arc as one option, not a law.

## 4. Sound that carries meaning (phonosemantics)

This is the deepest lever in the document. Sounds are **not** a neutral envelope for meaning; many of them *carry* meaning on their own, below the dictionary level. Used well, this lets you make a line's **sound agree with its sense** — the most powerful, least obvious form of word choice.

### 4.1 The bouba/kiki effect

Show people two shapes — one rounded blob, one jagged star — and two nonsense words, *bouba* and *kiki*. Across cultures, people overwhelmingly call the blob *bouba* and the star *kiki*. The reason is acoustic: **voiced sounds and rounded back vowels carry low-frequency energy and read as round, soft, large**; **voiceless stops and high front vowels carry high-frequency energy and read as sharp, spiky, small.** The mapping is real and largely pre-linguistic.

### 4.2 The frequency code (the master tool)

The **frequency code** generalizes bouba/kiki into a single rule:

> **The characteristic acoustic frequency of a speech sound maps *inversely* onto perceived size — and, by extension, onto brightness, sharpness, speed, and lightness vs. their opposites.**

- **Small / bright / sharp / quick / light** → **high front vowels** (*ee /i/, ih /ɪ/, ay /eɪ/, eh /ɛ/*) + **voiceless / sharp consonants** (*t, k, p, s*) + high pitch.
- **Big / dark / heavy / slow / looming** → **low back vowels** (*ah /ɑ/, aw /ɔ/, oh /oʊ/, oo /u/*) + **voiced sonorants** (*m, n, l, r, b, g, d*) + low pitch.

This isn't arbitrary. Offered nonsense words like *mil* and *mal* and asked which names a bigger table, people choose the /a/-word for "large" up to ~96% of the time. Big bodies have long vocal tracts that resonate low; small bodies resonate high — and animals exploit this, dropping pitch to seem threatening and raising it to seem small. Your listener carries that instinct.

**How to use it:** tune a line's emotional temperature *purely through vowel and consonant selection*, independent of what the words denote. Want it tiny, glittering, fast? Load it with *ee/ih* and *t/k/s*. Want it vast, slow, ominous? Load it with *oh/aw/oo* and *m/n/l/r*. When sound and sense agree, fluency spikes and the line feels *inevitable*. (See Worked Example 2.)

### 4.3 Phonesthemes (sound clusters with built-in meaning)

A **phonestheme** is a sub-word sound chunk that carries a recurring meaning without being a true prefix or suffix. They are tendencies, not rules, but they pull:

| Cluster | Tends to mean | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **gl-** | light, vision, shine | glow, gleam, glint, glitter, glisten, glance |
| **sl-** | slippery / pejorative | slime, slug, slush, sloppy, slick, slither |
| **sn-** | nose, contempt | sniff, snore, snout, sneer, snarl, snub |
| **fl-** | flickering light / motion | flicker, flutter, flap, flit, flash, flare, flow |
| **gr-** | low, harsh sound / displeasure | growl, grumble, groan, grind, gruff, grim |
| **cr- / -ack** | sudden breakage, impact | crack, crash, crunch, crackle |
| **-ash** | violent contact | crash, smash, bash, splash, clash, dash, lash |
| **-ump** | heavy rounded mass | lump, bump, hump, stump, clump, plump, rump |
| **tw-** | twist, turn | twist, twirl, tweak, twine, twitch |
| **-oom / -oon** | large, resonant (low back vowel) | boom, zoom, gloom, doom, loom |

Threading a phonestheme through the load-bearing words of a line creates a **subterranean unity** the listener feels without naming — and if it matches your meaning, sound and sense reinforce each other.

### 4.4 Ideophones and onomatopoeia

The strongest form of sound-meaning is **onomatopoeia / ideophones** — words that *are* their sound or sensation (*buzz, hiss, thud, click, splash, murmur*). Some languages are extraordinarily rich in them; Japanese mimetics are the textbook case (*kirakira* = a sparkle, *dokidoki* = a pounding heart, *fuwafuwa* = soft and fluffy). When you need maximum sound-sense fusion, reach for the ideophone.

### 4.5 An honest caveat

Judgments about which whole *languages* or words are "beautiful" are heavily contaminated by **familiarity and even cultural prejudice** — "euphony" has often been a proxy for what a listener was raised on. The *specific, acoustically-grounded* effects above (bouba/kiki, the frequency code, phonesthemes) are robust and cross-cultural and worth using. But don't over-essentialize "ugly" vs. "pretty" sounds in the abstract. In practice, **fit-to-meaning** and **freshness** matter as much as any raw sonic quality.

## 5. The sung line

The moment a lyric is **sung**, an extra layer of physics kicks in, and ignoring it is the most common reason a lyric that *reads* great *sings* badly.

### 5.1 Pitch lives on vowels

When you sustain a note, you are sustaining a **vowel**. The vocal tract is most open during vowels, so they carry the pitch, the volume, and nearly all the tone. **Consonants interrupt the airflow** — they are the rhythmic punctuation *between* sung pitches. A held note is a held vowel with consonants clipped onto its front and back. Every sustained note *is* that ringing vowel: the syllable struck just before the note holds is the one whose vowel rings on, so its vowel quality is what the listener sits inside.

### 5.2 The open-vowel rule (the "money note")

**Put your biggest, highest, longest, most-belted notes on open vowels** — *ah /ɑ/, aw /ɔ/, "a" /æ/, "ay" /eɪ/*, and to a degree *oh /oʊ/* and *eye /aɪ/*. **Avoid landing them on close vowels** — *ee /i/* and *oo /u/* — which are physically harder to sing powerfully or high; the narrow mouth shape fights resonance and strains the voice.

This is not folklore. Acoustic modeling of belting shows that for most vowels the full belt tone is available only over about a musical fifth, but on the open vowels **/ɑ/ and /ɔ/ that range stretches to a full octave.** The "money note" of a great chorus is almost always engineered so its climactic syllable lands on an open vowel. If your hook peaks on an *ee* or *oo*, consider rewriting the word.

That climactic syllable is easy to find: it sits on a high, long note — held out, or topped by a slide that peaks near the top of the range. Those are exactly the moments to place an open vowel on.

### 5.3 What singers do when the lyric won't cooperate

- **Vowel modification.** As pitch rises, singers reshape vowels toward more singable shapes. A high note written on *ee* gets colored toward *ih* or *eh* whether you like it or not — anticipate it.
- **Diphthong handling.** Words like *my, time, now, day* contain two vowels. Singers **sustain the first vowel** and add the glide only at the last instant (*ah…→ee* for *my*), because pure monophthongs hold a steadier tone. A long held note on a diphthong is really holding its first half.

### 5.4 Consonants as rhythmic articulation in a sung line

In melisma — one struck syllable trailed by sliding holds — the consonants vanish and you ride that single vowel across every waypoint. Everywhere else, consonants are the **rhythmic onsets** that place each note in time — so **stops** still give crisp rhythmic attack and **liquids/nasals** still glue notes together, exactly as in speech. They simply no longer carry the sustain.

> **Design implication:** decide *early* whether a line is rapped, spoken, or sung — and if sung, where its long notes fall. The long notes are the held notes, the high pitches, and any melisma; the short, sharp moments are the rhythmic attacks. Put open vowels under the former and crisp consonants on the latter.

## 6. Sonic devices, by layer

Sonic devices operate at different **layers** of language. **Combining devices from different layers** gives density without redundancy; **stacking devices on the same layer** gives noise.

### Layer 1 — Sub-syllabic (consonants and vowels)

- **Alliteration** — initial-consonant repetition: *slap, slip, slope*.
- **Consonance** — consonant repetition *anywhere* in the word: *pack / clip / luck*.
- **Assonance** — vowel repetition: *cold cup, slow sip*.
- **Sibilance** — specifically S/SH/Z repetition for hiss texture.
- **Plosive emphasis** — clustering stops on stressed beats for punch.

### Layer 2 — Syllable (rhyme)

Rhyme is the syllable-level layer of sonic repetition. It is a *graded* tool, not a single move, and it carries its own logic of closeness and resolution — see **`rhymingWords.md`** for the full catalog (perfect, family, slant, assonance, consonance, multis, mosaic), rhyme position, and when to loosen or withhold a rhyme.

### Layer 3 — Word and phrase (lexical repetition)

- **Anaphora** — repeating a word/phrase at the *start* of consecutive lines.
- **Epistrophe** — repeating it at the *end* of consecutive lines.
- **Refrain / hook word (anchor word)** — a single word that returns at structurally important positions. This is *not* alliteration — it operates on a different layer: whole-word repetition processes as *meaning coming back*, not a sound being beaten, so it doesn't fatigue the ear the way over-alliteration does. You can build a whole verse around one anchor word used three or four times while everything around it does different sonic work.
- **Mirror phrasing / parallelism** — the same syntactic shape across lines.
- **Chiasmus / antimetabole** — the same words reversed ("ask not what your country…"). Highly fluent and memorable.

### Layer 4 — Line and verse (structural)

- **Couplet pairing** — every two lines speak to each other.
- **Caesura** — a deliberate mid-line pause.
- **Line-length variation** — a short line for emphasis, a long line for setup.

> **The cardinal rule of layering:** combine devices from *different* layers. Alliteration + word repetition + slant rhyme = three independent dimensions of interest, none fighting. Alliteration + alliteration + alliteration = one dimension hammered to death.

## 7. Repetition and catchiness

"Catchy" is not mystical. Research on **earworms** points to concrete, reproducible features, and two of them are squarely about repetition:

- **Contiguous repetition is the single most earworm-inducing feature.** A fragment that repeats *immediately, back-to-back* sticks hardest. Don't space your hook out; loop it.
- **Mere exposure does real work.** Familiarity *is* liking; a hook earns affection partly by simply recurring — the deepest argument for a strong, repeated chorus, and a direct consequence of the fluency principle in §1.

The other levers of catchiness are covered where they belong: a hook's **singability** (open vowels under long notes, low articulatory load, a familiar contour with one twist) in §3 and §5; **anchor words** and the rhetorical schemes (anaphora, epistrophe, chiasmus) as lexical repetition in §6; and the way an *odd* number of repeats reads as slightly unstable in `prosodyMeter.md`.

## 8. The discipline of placement

This is what most distinguishes design from writing. Devices are not decorations to sprinkle uniformly. They are **weight**, and weight has to be *placed*.

### 8.1 Negative space

The unmarked words in a line are not filler. They are the **runway** that lets the marked words take off. A neutral, texture-free opening is what makes a clustered, loaded payoff *pop* when it arrives.

If every word is loaded, none of them registers. **The signal needs noise around it to read as signal.** This is the single most common mistake in amateur lyrics: trying to make *every* word "work." Every word working = no word standing out.

### 8.2 Devices placed, not spread

Decide *where* in the line you want the impact, then put the device **there.** Not before, not throughout. *There.* A strong line often has a neutral opening (runway), a loaded middle or back half (where one device clusters), and a clean payoff (where a single rhyme or stop lands). Three different things at three positions beats the same thing smeared across the whole line.

### 8.3 Density isn't depth

One well-placed sonic effect outperforms five competing ones. When tempted to add a device, ask: *is it doing work the existing devices aren't?* If it is on the **same layer** as something already present, you are probably *weakening* the original. The hardest skill in this craft is **leaving things plain.**

### 8.4 Put the heaviest sound where the meaning peaks

The loudest sonic moment of a line should coincide with its most important word. When the **sound climax** and the **meaning climax** land together, the line feels inevitable; when misaligned, the ear is pulled one way and the mind another.

### 8.5 Lead into the hit

A hit is an accent, and an accent needs a **run-up**. The syllable immediately *before* the hit should end on an **open or continuant sound** — a vowel, or a nasal or liquid (m, n, l, r) — so the voice carries momentum across the seam and *into* the accented syllable. End that approach syllable on a **hard stop or a consonant cluster** (*cold* -ld, *clouds* -ds, *act* -kt) and you drop a wall exactly where the line should be lifting: the airflow resets, and the hit has to restart from a dead stop instead of riding in. This is §2.5.3's cross-syllable-junction rule aimed at the one seam that matters most — the one feeding the accent. Swapping a hard-ending approach word for one that ends open (*clouds* → *we*, *cold* → *I'm*) is frequently the single change that makes a hit *land* instead of *clunk*. (It has a prosodic twin: that approach syllable should also be metrically *weak* — a function word that cedes the stress, not a heavy content word that clashes with the accent; see `prosodyMeter.md` §5.) Diagnose it the only reliable way: sing the two syllables across the bar and feel whether the mouth glides through or has to stop and reload.

## 9. Worked examples

Each line below is written to isolate one technique. Build your own the same way: pick the principle first, then engineer a line that demonstrates *only* that.

### Example 1 — Plosive punch + short-vowel anchor (spoken/rapped)

> **"Snap the lock, pack the box, knock it back."**

- **Vowel anchor:** low/back short vowels throughout — *snap /æ/, lock /ɒ/, pack /æ/, box /ɒ/, knock /ɒ/, back /æ/*. The mouth keeps returning to two neighboring postures, so the line feels *anchored*.
- **Consonance (stops everywhere):** *sn-, -ck, p-, -x, kn-, -ck, b-, -ck*. Built of plosives — pure percussion.
- **Stop landing:** ends on /k/ (*back*). The line *snaps shut*; nothing trails.
- **Use it for:** aggression, momentum, a punch-in line over a hard beat.

### Example 2 — The frequency code, two ways (sound matching sense)

Same neutral content — *"something moved and made a sound"* — rendered twice:

> **Small / bright / quick:** *"a thin little flicker, a quick silver tick"*

- High front vowels, voiceless/sharp consonants, and the **fl-** phonestheme (*flicker*). It *sounds* tiny and fast because every sonic choice agrees with "small."

> **Big / dark / slow:** *"a low rolling rumble that moans and rolls on"*

- Low back vowels, voiced sonorants, and the **gr-/-umble** phonestheme. It *sounds* vast and slow.
- **The lesson:** the dictionary meaning barely changed; *vowel and consonant selection* did all the emotional work.

### Example 3 — The sung "money note" (open vowel under the big note)

Two candidate chorus endings, both meaning roughly *"I'm staying"*:

- **Weak to sing:** a hook climbing to its highest, longest note on *…leave* (/i/). The close front vowel strains and thins at the top.
- **Strong to sing:** the same climb landing on *…stay* (/eɪ/) or *…all* (/ɔ/) — an open vowel that opens, rings, and belts.
- **Design move:** write the *line* so the syllable carrying the climactic note is an open vowel. If your draft peaks on *ee* or *oo*, swap the word. (And if the note's on a diphthong like *stay*, the singer holds the *first* half, *eh→*, clipping the glide on at the end.)
- **The craft point:** the climax is that high, held syllable — a high note stretched across a long hold, where the open /eɪ/ of *stay* rings. Put the close /i/ of *leave* in the exact same spot and the belt strands. The note is identical; only the vowel you place on it decides whether it sings.

### Example 4 — Layering done right (three layers, each placed once)

> **"You talked all night, said nothing at all —**
> **now the lock clicks shut down the empty hall."**

- **Layer 1 (sub-syllabic):** Line 1 anchors hard on **/ɔ/** — *talked, all, all* — which also doubles as **lexical repetition** of *all*. Line 2 clusters its consonants in the *middle* (*lock clicks shut*) and keeps the opening ("now the") plain.
- **Layer 2 (rhyme):** *all / hall* — a **perfect** rhyme, chosen because the moment is *resolved* (the door is shut). And *hall* returns to the **/ɔ/** anchor from line 1, so rhyme and vowel anchor are the same color — maximum cohesion.
- **Layer 3 (structural):** a syntactic turn from *"you talked…"* (the past) to *"now…"* (the aftermath). The shape carries the story.
- **Placement:** neutral runway → mid-line consonant cluster → perfect-rhyme payoff on the downbeat. Three different things at three positions; none fighting.

### Example 5 — Reduplication hook (the babble default)

> **"oh-oh, here we go-oh"**

- Pure **CV reduplication** on open vowels: the lowest-effort, most-singable, most language-independent shape there is (§3.3) — and **contiguous repetition** (§7), the top earworm feature.
- It carries almost no semantic load, which is the point: a vocable chant is a *pure* fluency object. Bolt it to the front or back of a chorus as the part everyone shouts.

## 10. Checklists

### 10.1 Mouth-mechanics check
- [ ] Where does the mouth start and end? Is there a clear journey, or does it bounce randomly?
- [ ] Any large place jumps (velar → bilabial) you didn't intend? Can adjacent gestures overlap, or do they force resets?
- [ ] Any clusters that violate the sonority rise? Are they doing useful work, or just tripping the tongue?
- [ ] Does the line end on a **stop** (punch) or a **sustain/open vowel** (open)? Is that the effect you want?

### 10.2 Sound-meaning check
- [ ] Do the vowels match the feeling? (High-front = small/bright/quick; low-back = big/dark/slow.)
- [ ] Is there a phonestheme you could thread through the key words to reinforce the sense?
- [ ] Is the *sound climax* sitting on the *meaning climax*?

### 10.3 Sung-delivery check
- [ ] Do the longest / highest / belted notes — the held notes, the high pitches, any melisma — land on **open vowels** (*ɑ, ɔ, æ, eɪ*), not on *ee/oo*?
- [ ] Are crisp consonants on the accented hits and sustain-friendly sounds (vowels, nasals, liquids) under the held notes?
- [ ] Any held note stuck on a diphthong you should be aware of?

### 10.4 Layering & placement check
- [ ] How many *different* devices are present, and how many of the *same*? (Different across layers = good; same on one layer = noise.)
- [ ] What's the negative space? Are plain words *deliberately* holding rhythm so the loud words can speak?
- [ ] Are two devices fighting for the same beat?
- [ ] Does each hit have an open run-up — does the syllable *before* it end on a vowel, nasal, or liquid, not a hard stop or cluster? (§8.5)

### 10.5 The out-loud test
- [ ] Say it three times. Does it speed up or slow down on repeat? (Speeding up = fluent; snagging = friction.)
- [ ] Where does your mouth want to stumble — is that a feature or a bug?
- [ ] **Whisper it.** Does the rhythm still work with the pitch stripped away?
- [ ] If it's a hook: do you involuntarily want to say it again? If not, it isn't a hook yet.

## 11. Common failure modes

**Stacking the same device.** Every word starts with the same consonant; every word ends in *-ack*. One layer carrying all the weight; the ear fatigues. *Fix:* swap half the device-words for runway words, or move a device to a different layer.

**No anchor at all.** Random words that merely fit the meter, with no recurring vowel or consonant. *Fix:* pick one element to repeat, even loosely.

**Sound fighting sense.** Bright, spiky high-front vowels under a heavy, mournful lyric (or vice versa). The frequency code working *against* you. *Fix:* re-pick vowels/consonants so the sound agrees with the meaning.

**Closed vowels on the big note.** A soaring sung climax stranded on *ee* or *oo*. It strains and thins. *Fix:* rewrite the word so the climactic note sits on an open vowel.

**Soft endings on punch lines.** Ending a line that *wants to hit* on a fricative or liquid blunts it. *Fix:* land on a stop (P, T, K, B, D, G).

**Density mistaken for depth.** Five devices crammed in, none breathing. *Fix:* cut to the one best-placed effect; trust the plainness around it.

**Workshopped feel.** Every word visibly chosen, every device showing its work. *Fix:* leave some words plain on purpose. Trust the listener.

# Sound Lookup — Quick Reference

*Two at-a-glance tables for the sound pass. Reach for these when you want a specific effect;

This is a lookup annex, not a read-through. Use it when you are tuning a line's sound and need to find — fast — which consonant transitions stay smooth, or which vowels and consonants carry a given feeling. Both tables are tendencies, not laws: fit-to-meaning and freshness still rule, and any of these can be broken on purpose.

## Consonant transitions, easiest → hardest

How fluently one sound moves to the next. Harder transitions are not *forbidden* — they are deliberate tension, and should be chosen rather than stumbled into. (The underlying mechanics — articulatory distance, overlap, and the sonority rise — are in `phoneticAppeal.md`, §2–3.)

**Smooth**
- Vowel → liquid → vowel (*I'll* sliding between vowels)
- Stop → liquid → vowel (CL, BL, GR, PR…)
- Same place of articulation across a syllable boundary (*good time*: D→T, both alveolar)
- S + stop (SP, ST, SK) — the recognized fluent exception
- Glide → vowel (W, Y ramping in)

**Workable**
- Stop → vowel → stop (CVC, the basic syllable)
- Fricative → vowel → stop
- Adjacent places hopping in one direction (K → T → P, back-to-front)
- Nasal → vowel (always easy; you can even hum into it)

**Effortful (use on purpose)**
- Stop → stop across a syllable boundary (*act-ed* needs a reset)
- Large back-and-forth place jumps (P → K → P)
- Three-plus consonant clusters where sonority doesn't rise (*sixths, twelfths*)
- Same fricative repeated across a seam (*this song*: S→S slurs)
- Same/adjacent sibilants alternating (S↔SH), the classic tongue-twister engine

## The frequency-code cheat sheet

When you want a line to *feel* a certain way, load it with these vowels, consonants, and phonesthemes. (The frequency code itself — why high-front/voiceless reads small-and-bright and low-back/voiced reads big-and-dark — is explained in `phoneticAppeal.md`, §4.)

| You want it to feel…               | Vowels                                        | Consonants                                  | Sample phonesthemes                         |
|---                                 |---                                            |---                                          |---                                          |
| small, bright, sharp, quick, light | high front: *ee /i/, ih /ɪ/, ay /eɪ/, eh /ɛ/* | voiceless/sharp: *t, k, p, s, sh*           | *fl-* (flicker), *gl-* (glint), *tw-* (twitch) |
| big, dark, heavy, slow, vast       | low/back: *ah /ɑ/, aw /ɔ/, oh /oʊ/, oo /u/*   | voiced sonorants: *m, n, ng, l, r, b, g, d* | *gr-* (growl), *-oom* (boom), *-ump* (lump) |
| punchy, percussive, hard           | short: *æ, ɪ, ʌ, ɒ*                           | stops/affricates: *p, t, k, b, d, g, ch, j* | *cr-/-ack* (crack), *-ash* (smash)          |
| smooth, flowing, tender            | long/open + diphthongs                        | liquids/nasals/glides: *l, r, m, n, w, y*   | *sl-* (slow-adjacent), *fl-* (flow)         |
| hissing, airy, uneasy              | (any)                                         | sibilants/fricatives: *s, sh, z, f, h*      | *sl-* (slime), *sn-* (sneer)                |

For singing, cross-reference: the **open vowels** (*ɑ, ɔ, æ, eɪ*) that carry big notes overlap with the **big/dark** and **smooth** rows — which is why power ballads live there.

## Glossary

- **Alliteration** — repetition of initial consonant sounds.
- **Anaphora / Epistrophe** — repetition of a word/phrase at the start / end of consecutive lines.
- **Assonance** — repetition of vowel sounds.
- **Bouba/kiki effect** — cross-cultural tendency to match round shapes to low-frequency sounds and spiky shapes to high-frequency sounds.
- **Chiasmus / antimetabole** — repeating words in reversed order.
- **Coarticulation** — the overlapping of neighboring speech gestures; the basis of "flow."
- **Consonance** — repetition of consonant sounds anywhere in the word.
- **Contiguous repetition** — a fragment repeated immediately, back-to-back; the strongest earworm feature.
- **Frequency code** — acoustic frequency maps inversely to perceived size, and onto bright/sharp vs. dark/heavy.
- **Manner of articulation** — *how* airflow is obstructed (stop, fricative, affricate, nasal, liquid, glide).
- **Mere-exposure effect** — repeated exposure increases liking.
- **Phonestheme** — a sub-word sound cluster with a recurring meaning (*gl-*, *sl-*, *-ump*).
- **Place of articulation** — *where* in the vocal tract a consonant is made.
- **Processing fluency** — the ease of taking something in; experienced as pleasure, memorability, and perceived truth.
- **Sibilance** — repetition of S/SH/Z hiss.
- **Sonority** — the inherent loudness/openness of a sound.
- **Sonority Sequencing Principle** — sonority rises into the vowel and falls after it.
- **Vocable** — a sung nonsense syllable (*la, na, oh, doo*).
- **Voicing** — whether the vocal folds vibrate during a consonant.
- **Vowel modification** — a singer's adjustment of a vowel for resonance, especially up high.

---

*The principles are guidelines, not rules. Every great lyric breaks something on this list — but it does it deliberately.*
