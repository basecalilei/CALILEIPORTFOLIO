## 1. The technology — the wax matrix

- The core asset is not a list of formulas but a **generative system**: a wax matrix that can structurally support and **evenly suspend a dry load of powdered ingredients**.
- Controlling the dry load gives full control over every characteristic of the finish: color, sheen (gloss ↔ matte), hand-feel (tacky ↔ chalky), opacity, effects (subtle ↔ loud).
- Formulation variables under direct control: melt point, migration rate, optical finish, flexibility, particle distribution, long-term tactile feel.
- Demonstrated finish directions: dry matte chalk bloom · stress-fracturing finishes · clean/uniform-until-creased · industrial stiffness · archival dullness · ceramic bloom · reactive metallic shimmer.
- The formula space is effectively **unlimited** — any finish can be built on demand. Any past formula can be re-made on demand (this is what makes the drop/graduation model work).
- Ingredient classes: structural waxes, natural conditioners, oil softeners, additive surface modifiers.
- **Every formula waterproofs the fabric**, regardless of finish.
- Defensibility (founder's honest read): a competent competitor could build their own fabric wax and eventually crack dry-loading. The moat is head start + brand/content execution, not the chemistry. This is why the brand work is load-bearing.

## 3. Product knowledge

### The bar & how it bonds
- The bar is activated by **heat or friction**. Bars vary in hardness, scent, color, and texture by formula.
- Slight surface bloom or separation on an unused bar is **normal, not a defect** (support fact).
- The wax **binds into the fabric** — absorbed into the surface, moving with the textile — rather than sealing over it like a film coating.

### Application methods
1. **Rub-in** (no equipment): rub the bar directly onto clean, dry fabric with pressure and short strokes; spread/melt with palm, cloth, or sponge; optional low-heat hair dryer / heat gun to deepen penetration. Produces a good result with its own character — *different* from the brushed look, not worse (founder-confirmed).
2. **Brush-on** (best coverage and evenness): melt the bar in a double boiler or wax pot (never direct flame), brush on in smooth layers section by section, apply heat to help absorption, optionally buff or layer. This is the method behind hero content.
3. **Palm-warm** (soft blends, spot work): warm the bar between palms or against fabric, massage in by hand.
- Melted waxes can be mixed to create hybrid custom formulas.
- **Cure: 12–48 hours** depending on formula and thickness; the item may feel stiff/tacky/damp at first — temporary. Cure fully before heavy use to avoid transfer. Once set, the surface can be buffed smooth or left textured.

### Effects on fabric — physical (present in all formulas; degree varies)
- **Surface thickness:** wax fills micro-gaps in the weave → denser, slightly heavier hand.
- **Structure vs. drape:** formulation sets where the fabric lands on the spectrum from added stiffness/body to preserved flexibility.
- **Weather resistance:** all formulas increase water + wind resistance; tunable toward max hydrophobicity or a breathability balance.
- **Abrasion resistance:** tunable — deflect wear, or permit intentional wear breakdown.
- **Thermal behavior:** some formulas insulate (retain heat); others stay breathable/cool.
- **Break-in:** rigid waxes break into sharp creases and stay sharp; soft waxes mellow and spread evenly with use.

### Effects on fabric — optical (highly formula- and method-dependent)
- **Saturation:** can intensify (richer, deeper color) or lighten (milky/matte veil). The most variable effect.
- **Value:** oil-rich blends darken the base fabric; dry waxes with opacifiers lighten/dull it.
- **Hue:** resins, oils, and pigments can shift hue; formulas can be deliberately tinted.
- **Reflectivity:** spans semi-gloss/sheen ↔ fully matte; rubbing/brushing technique exaggerates either.
- **Wax break / crease accentuation:** creases micro-fracture the wax into high/low tones; strongest in stiffen-then-break formulas. This is the core patina mechanism.
- **Wear mapping:** high-friction zones fade/crease faster than protected zones → personal high/low contrast over time; formulas range from slow/even aging to fast "fade maps."
- **Texture visualization:** high-solids formulas highlight weave structure; brush strokes or hand-rub patterns can remain visible or be neutralized by method.
- **Bloom:** natural waxes (beeswax, carnauba) migrate to the surface and crystallize into a faint chalky veil, triggered by temperature/humidity swings. Buff out or keep — legitimate either way.

### Patina retention mechanics
Definition: the treated fabric's ability to retain physical/visual traces of use (creases, scratches, pressure marks, wear paths) instead of rebounding to like-new. Mechanism: fabric + wax **capture** deformation, **lock it in** (wax displacement, fracture, compression, surface shift), and **prevent full recovery**.

- **Crease holding** (physical): fabric stays physically creased after folding/flexing; strongest in high-stiffness or layered applications.
- **Crease ghosting** (optical): fracture lines catch light differently, leaving a visible "ghost" even if the fabric physically relaxes; prominent in medium-hardness waxes.
- **Compression memory:** flattened zones retain a shadow or feel (seat fades, strap indentations); depends on oil content and fabric density.
- **Edge retention:** fraying, folds, and scuffs along edges become permanent visual markers; strongest in high-friction, medium-rigidity formulas.
- **Shape memory:** the item holds its stored 3D form even when not in use; strong with high-stiffness wax on tight weaves.
- **Scratch retention:** light–medium scratches show as lighter marks, especially on darker semi-gloss finishes; some formulas re-burnish out, others lock in.

Tuning variables: harder wax → stronger crease hold + ghosting · more oil → softer memory, more shadow retention · thicker layer → stronger edge/scratch memory · rubbed application → more texture memory (dipped → more even) · dense weaves hold marks better than loose · repeated stress in one zone → deeper visible patina.

### Durability (founder's real-world numbers)
- Boots, one good coat: **~6 months** at ~5 wears/week. Less under daily hard use (construction-site tier).
- Light-rotation fashion consumer (many shoes): wax once, **~2 years**.
- Consequence: this is a transform-once product, not a maintenance treadmill. Repeat revenue = new colors/items, not rewax.

### Footwear specifics (the beachhead facts)
- Wax can fully recolor footwear, including **soles** — wax sits on the sole surface rather than absorbing, and holds.
- Solves a real problem: dye fails on soles and takes unevenly across mixed materials.
- First coat lands a little uneven → immediate weathered look (a feature, presented as one).
- Patina develops over months of wear — the bonus layer.

### Behavior over time (time-based behaviors)
- **Wax migration:** wax molecules gradually move within the fiber structure → light blooming, darkening, or texture shift; faster in high-oil/soft blends, warm environments, tight storage. (Bloom, the visible result, is covered under optical effects.)
- **Wax transfer:** during cure or in hot conditions, wax can transfer to other surfaces (car seats, bags, other clothes) — more likely with heavy application. Prevention: full cure, buff excess, heat-set.
- **Drying out:** over ~6–18 months a wax layer loses flexibility, can dull or feel chalky — refresh territory (relevant mostly for heavy-use cases).
- Rewaxing restores weather resistance, color depth/sheen, and surface behavior; leaving layers to age instead develops a unique wear profile.

### Reapplication & staging (raw technique info)
- Reapply when: water stops beading (spot rewax) · surface looks faded/dry (light coat + buff) · more stiffness wanted (second coat or harder formula) · refreshing the look (rub-in over faded zones) · ahead of heavy use or rain (pre-treat friction zones).
- Staging techniques: apply heavier wax in stress zones for stronger retention · leave zones unwaxed for high-contrast fade maps · crease or edge-rub while the wax is still warm to set intentional wear paths.

### Care (honest version — this is the maintenance wall)
- Waxed items are **wash-hostile**. Machine wash warm/hot (melts wax out), tumble drying even cold (drum friction abrades), normal detergent (emulsifies/strips), dry cleaning (solvents dissolve), soaking (loosens from the inside out), and steam/ironing (overheats, pushes wax out of the weave) all strip or ruin the wax.
- Acceptable methods: **(1) Spot clean** — cold water only, no soap, soft sponge/brush, air dry shaded. **(2) If truly needed** (skin-contact soft goods): cold delicate cycle, inside-out, crumpled in a mesh net bag, little/no mild detergent, hang dry shaded; inspect and re-wax patchy areas. (Inside-out + net minimizes surface friction; cold + gentle minimizes wax disruption.)
- A stripped finish is recoverable by full rewax, but accumulated patina history is lost.
- **Strategic consequence:** this wall is exactly why the brand leads with never-washed accessories. For garment content/customers, be honest about care up front — it filters correctly.

### Troubleshooting quick answers
- *Too stiff* → thinner layer or softer formula next time; softens with wear.
- *Streaks* → gently reheat and buff; add wax to even coverage.
- *Wax rubbed off* → longer cure; heat-set; layer.
- *Scratches* → normal behavior; buff if unwanted.
- *Dusty/muddy* → spot clean only; never soak.
- *Odor/body oils* → cold net-bag wash, inside out.
- *Washed it wrong, finish patchy/gone* → fabric is fine; rewax rebuilds the finish from scratch; teach correct care going forward.
- *Asks about regular machine washing* → honest answer: possible, but wax loss is likely.
- *Wants low-maintenance* → recommend a lower-wax formula or minimal wax zones.
