# comboBounce

*How a combo's placement against the anchor creates bounce — the timing half of `lineCombo.md`.*

**Version:** 0.2.0
**Status:** Draft — the HIT and PLACEMENT layers, the two hard rules, the stress/quantity rules for figures landing a hit, and meaning–placement alignment are in. Two worked examples (duck-dive, broke-a-sweat). **Source-neutral by design** — the concepts hold for any fixed pulse; Brooklyn drill is named only as where they were isolated. *Where* this writer sets these dials is **not** here — it lives in `voiceStyle.md` §6.
**Part of:** `calileiStyle`

---

## 0. What this doc owns

`lineCombo.md` builds the combo as a unit of *meaning and rhyme* — call → response, the two lines, the landing, hit-anchored rhyme. `comboBounce.md` covers the **same unit in time**: how it is placed against the instrumental pulse, and how that placement makes it move.

Everything here rests on one claim:

> **The anchor is fixed. The combo is not. Bounce is the offset between them.**

The anchor (`calileiNotation` L8) is the instrumental's repeating pulse — a fixed grid of expected hits that does not move. The combo is laid *over* that grid and **does not have to sit flush on it**. The push-and-pull between the moving voice and the fixed pulse — where a line opens relative to a hit, where it lands, how many syllables it crams between hits — *is* the bounce. Lock the voice flat onto the pulse and the bounce dies; weave it around the pulse and the voice glides.

(`calileiNotation` G3 says this in one sentence — "bounce comes from how lines, syllables, and hits are arranged *around* the anchor." This doc is the grammar that cashes that sentence out.)

> **Source — Brooklyn drill.** This bounce was isolated from Brooklyn drill, studied for its rhythm. The mechanism below is written to be **source-neutral**: it holds for any fixed pulse, wherever the anchor sits. Drill only supplies the pattern used in the worked examples — the anchor on the **downbeat of beat 1**, its period varying (every bar, or every other). The **3 is not a second anchor** — it's the even midpoint between 1-anchors, where exposed hits like to land (see §2). Other anchor placements change the canvas, not the grammar.

## 1. The two layers, recapped (borrowed, not redefined)

This doc assumes `calileiNotation` and works on top of it. It does **not** re-spec the glyphs.

- **anchor** `#` — the instrumental's repeating hit; a **silent reference**, scaffolding only (L8). It is **the single slot the listener most strongly expects a hit on** — set by the beat, usually the downbeat of the 1, with a period that can vary (every bar, every other bar). Where it falls is the instrumental's business (G2); this doc takes it as given and works the voice against it.
- **hit** `*` — a **vocal** accent, an emphasized landing in the flow (L9). A separate layer from the anchor.

The anchor (instrumental) and the hit (vocal) are **independent**. Their alignment or offset is the whole game. `comboBounce` is the study of that offset at the scale of the combo.

> **One word, one meaning here.** Across the rhythmic docs (`comboBounce.md`, `calileiNotation.md`), *anchor* always means **this** — the `#` instrumental pulse, the fixed rhythmic thing the vocal leans on and places against. The craft docs borrow the word for the same *fixed-handhold* intuition on other layers, always **qualified**: the returning **hook** (`callResponse.md`), a recurring **image** (`vividDiction.md`), a recurring **vowel or word** (`phoneticAppeal.md`). Same metaphor, different layer — none of those is the rhythmic anchor, and where the two could be confused, this is the definition that holds.

## 2. HIT — the vocal accent, read against the anchor

A hit lands somewhere relative to the anchor, and *where* decides how it hits:

- **anchorHit** — an accent landing **on** the anchor (the strongest-expectation slot, §1). Impact by **reinforcement**: the anchor lends its weight, so the hit feels heavy, grounded, inevitable.
- **bareHit** — an accent landing **off** the anchor. Impact by **exposure**: nothing is under it, so the voice carries it alone — it owns the frequency space, contrasts dynamically against the backed hits, and syncopates against the pulse. *The voice becomes its own drum.*

These are two ways to make a hit land hard — reinforcement vs. exposure. A passage that is all anchorHits is grounded but can sit flat; exposing the right hits gives snap and surprise. The mix is a dial.

**Where a bareHit wants to be.** A bareHit can go anywhere, but it **gravitates to the even midpoint between anchors** — the spot the ear reads as the natural offbeat counterweight to the pulse. Anchor on the downbeat of the 1 each bar → bareHits sit best on the **downbeat of the 3.** This is *why* the 3 feels good even though it is not an anchor: it's the midpoint, not a second pulse. Two common uses:

- **midpoint bareHit** — sits at the even gap (the 3), a standalone syncopated event owning its own space against the pulse.
- **add-on bareHit** — hangs right off an anchorHit as a tag, a beat or a 16th late, *decorating* the downbeat hit it trails from rather than occupying the midpoint. (The repeated *yet* in §7's broke-a-sweat combo is this kind.)

## 3. PLACEMENT — how a unit drapes over its hits

Placement is set on **two independent axes**. Pick one from each.

**open axis** — where the unit *begins* relative to a hit:
- `hardOpen` — begins **on** a hit.
- `floatOpen` — begins **slightly before** a hit, gliding through it.

**land axis** — where the unit *ends* relative to a hit:
- `hardLand` — ends **on** a hit.
- `floatLand` — ends **slightly after** a hit, trailing past it.

The four resulting shapes:

| shape | feel |
|---|---|
| `hardOpen` · `hardLand` | punchy · bookended |
| `hardOpen` · `floatLand` | plant, then release |
| `floatOpen` · `hardLand` | wind up, then strike |
| `floatOpen` · `floatLand` | pure drift |

**The shape carries the emotion.** It's a spectrum of coupling to the pulse: the more **hard** ends a passage uses, the more it reads as *insistence / passion*; the more **float** ends, the more it reads as *nonchalance / drift*. A combo is **built from a mix** of these line by line — flush and floating both in play, chosen per line for the feel. Neither end is a default or a fallback; `hardOpen` and `hardLand` are full citizens, not the safe option.

**Match the shape to the meaning.** The strongest combos don't just keep the grid felt — they pick the placement that *agrees with what the words are doing.* A nostalgic flex wants a **floatLand** (it trails off, wistful, already receding); a flat certain statement wants a **hardLand** (bookended, settled, no give). When the shape and the content say the same thing, the line lands twice. This is the gap between placement that merely *works* and placement that *means* — and it's the bar a finished combo should clear.

## 4. The hard rules

Two things are non-negotiable. Note what is *not* on the list: the **rhyme pattern**. Rhyme is the free variable — the place you keep it interesting (§6) — not a rule.

**Rule 1 — float only reads *as* float against landings.**
Keep enough `hardLand`s in a section to keep the grid felt. Strip them out and the pocket dissolves into formlessness — drift with nothing to drift against is just noise. The corollary is the payoff: a lone drift placed among hard landings lands *as a standout* **precisely because it refuses to.** The hard placements are what make the float legible; spend them to earn the drift.

**Rule 2 — every hit that fires must pay off.**
The rule is *not* that every 1 (or every midpoint) gets hit — most won't. The rule is that the hits you **do** take must be **satisfying** — each one should deliver a little hit of dopamine. A hit is the promise the grid makes to the ear; a limp or empty accent on a strong slot is a broken promise. So fire fewer hits and make each one count: a clean onset on the expected slot, a payoff word on it, the shape agreeing with the meaning (§3). You owe the listener nothing on the slots you skip — and a payoff on every slot you strike.

---

## 5. Pace — anchored vs. elastic

The hits are fixed; the **syllable count between them flexes.**

- **Anchored vs. elastic.** The hits are the fixed posts. **Pace** is how much you cram into the **fill** between two hits — not where the hits move. Dense fill = busy, urgent; sparse fill = open, patient. You change the feel by loading or emptying the gaps, never by dragging the posts.
- **The light approach.** The cell **before** a hit wants a weak, demotable syllable — a function word (*the, a, for, im-, like*) — so the accent **pops off** it cleanly instead of clashing with a competing stress. Set the table with a light syllable; let the hit be the heavy one.

---

## 6. The double-hit stress rule

When a **two-word (double) figure lands a hit**, the stress and the impact go to the **first onset**, which sits **on the downbeat**; the second word is an **unstressed trailer** that rides off it into the next slot.

> **DUCK** dive · **BUS** ride — the first onset takes the accent and the downbeat; *dive / ride* trail behind, unstressed.

This has a consequence for rhyme. The combo's hit-anchored rhyme (`lineCombo.md` §6, `rhymingWords.md`) binds on the **stressed downbeat onset** — even when the *trailing* word is the closer-sounding pair. In *duck dive / bus ride*, both halves rhyme, but the bind that carries the combo is **duck / bus** (the impact onsets on the 1), not *dive / ride* (the prettier trailer riding behind). The downbeat onset is the load-bearing rhyme; the trailer is a secondary thread on a non-hit slot.

**Quantity can stand in for closeness.** What a figure owes the hit is a clean **onset on the expected slot** — not a perfect rhyme. A fast, packed multi-syllable figure (*break-a-sweat*, *for-the-best*) can read *as* a strong rhyme by sheer **quantity**: three quick syllables sharing shape land satisfying even when the one matched vowel is buried in the trail. So a figure can pay off its hit two ways — by **quality** (a close rhyme on the stressed onset, *broke / go*) or by **quantity** (a dense triplet whose bulk reads as a bind). Both are legitimate; the non-negotiable is only that the onset starts on the slot.

## 7. Worked examples

**How to read this section.** The examples are a controlled experiment. The **baseline** sits at the *null* setting of every dial — anchor on the 1, anchorHits only, square placement, even pace, a plain rhyme on the hit. Every example after it is **the baseline with exactly one dial moved**, named at the top, so the effect of that single change is isolated. Read enough single-dial moves and the universal rules surface on their own — nobody asserts them; they're what's left standing across every variation.

> **Build status.** Baseline first, then the variation suite — populated *from real combos* as they're polished, each slotting in under whichever dial it spotlights. The duck-dive below is the first recast example (it opens up hit placement and the §6 figure rules at once); broke-a-sweat still predates the template and will be recast as the **wind-up-strike** case. Minimal single-dial studies (pure drift alone, one midpoint bareHit, pace, the add-on tag) come after.

### Baseline — flush on the pulse (zero offset)

The plainest possible combo: a hit on the downbeat of every 1, both lines bookended `hardOpen · hardLand`, nothing floated, nothing exposed, one clean rhyme on the landing. By the doc's own definition — *bounce is the offset between the combo and the anchor* — this combo has **no bounce**: the voice sits flush on the pulse. That is exactly its job. It is the reference zero; every other example departs from it, and the bounce you hear there *is* the departure.

```
|| *Best | in:the | room:_ | I'm:the ||
|| *most=A | _ | _ | _ ||
|| *Win | with:no | try:in' | I:just ||
|| *coast=A | _ | _ | _ ||
```

```
{ lineCombo
  [ Best in the room, I'm the most ]      L1 (call)
  [ Win with no tryin', I just coast ]    L2 (response)
}
anchor:  downbeat of the 1, every bar — a fixed, steady pulse
```

**The mechanics, in their null state.**

- **Anchor.** The same slot every bar — the downbeat of the 1, a steady four-bar pulse (§1). Nothing varies; the monotony is the point. (Every 1 is realized as a hit, so no `#` remains — L8.)
- **Hits — all anchorHits.** All four hits (*Best, most, Win, coast*) land *on* the 1, on the anchor, so every one is an **anchorHit** — pure reinforcement (§2). No bareHits, nothing exposed at the midpoint. The grid is maximally backed.
- **Placement — `hardOpen · hardLand`, both lines.** Each line *begins* on a hit (*Best*, *Win* — the first word **is** the downbeat onset, no pickup → `hardOpen`) and *ends* on a hit (*most*, *coast* on the next 1 → `hardLand`). The bookended, "punchy" shape (§3). Zero offset on either axis: the voice never glides in early, never trails out late.
- **Pace — neutral.** A moderate, even fill walks bar 1 down to the landing; then the line rests through bar 2 so the hit rings (§5). Nothing crammed, nothing starved.
- **Bind — plain, on the impact.** The combo rhymes **most / coast** (`=A`, perfect /oʊst/) on the two `hardLand` hits — the rhyme sits squarely on the downbeat onset, the simplest case of hit-anchored rhyme (§6). The opens (*Best, Win*) are content anchorHits, no rhyme.

**Why it's the zero.** Both hard rules are satisfied the *trivial* way: every landing is hard, so the grid is maximally felt (Rule 1 has nothing to hold up, because nothing floats); every hit pays off plainly (Rule 2). Meaning and shape agree — these are flat, certain brags, and certainty *wants* the bookended hardLand (§3), which is why a flush placement reads on-voice here instead of stiff: it's the voice at its most settled. The **light approach** sets up each landing (*I'm the* → **most**, *I just* → **coast**).

And it is, deliberately, a little **locked**: with no offset anywhere there's no glide and no bounce — words on a metronome. That flatness is the absence every following example fills. From here, move exactly one dial — float the open (glide *into* the hit), float the land (trail *past* it, and overflow the bar), drop a hit to the midpoint (a bareHit), hang a tag off a hit (an add-on bareHit), fire fewer hits (Rule 2 under pressure), cram or starve the fill (pace), swap the plain rhyme for a quantity-triplet or pull it off the hit (the rhyme as free variable), or mismatch shape and meaning. Each is one section; the baseline never changes, only the named dial does.

### The duck-dive combo — hits across the bar, and figures that pay off

**The move (vs. baseline).** The baseline put one anchorHit per line, flush on the 1, square both ends. This combo breaks all of that open at once: hits land **across all four beats**, most of them **bareHits**, packed into fast **multi-syllable figures**, with both lines floating (`floatOpen · floatLand`). It's the wide-open opposite of the baseline — its job is to show how much freedom the space actually has, before later examples isolate each dial one at a time. (`#` shown on the anchor row only; the realized grid carries no `#`, per L8.)

```
anchor:  || # | _ | _ | _ ||   (downbeat of the 1, every bar)

|| _ | _ | _ | _:_:I:can ||
|| *feel:the:shade=D:_ | _:im:ma | *dodge=C:it:like:a | *duck=A:dive=B:_:don't ||
|| *ev=C:en:know:ya | *name=D:_:here's:a | *dol=C:la:for:ya | *bus=A:ride=B ||
```

```
{ lineCombo
  [ I can feel the shade, imma dodge it like a duck dive ]      L1 (call)
  [ don't even know ya name, here's a dolla for ya bus ride ]   L2 (response)
}
```

**Where the hits land.** The anchor is still only the downbeat of the 1, and just two hits sit on it — *feel* (bar 2) and *even* (bar 3) — so only those two are **anchorHits**. The other five — *dodge, duck, name, dolla, bus* — fall on beats 2, 3, and 4: **bareHits**, exposed off the pulse. But notice what holds constant: every hit lands on a **beat-downbeat** (the head of a beat), never on an inner subdivision. That's the rule of thumb — *hits go anywhere, but mainly on the downbeats* — here, the downbeats of all four beats instead of only the 1. The combo runs mostly on exposure, which is a big part of why it feels so loose and fast.

**How each hit pays off (§6).** Rule 2 says every hit must land something satisfying. This combo pays three different ways, and not one of them is a plain single-syllable rhyme:

- **The double — `duck dive` / `bus ride`.** A solid double: the stressed first onset takes the hit and the beat (*DUCK*, *BUS*, /ʌ/ — thread A), the second word trails off it unstressed (*dive*, *ride*, /aɪ/ — thread B). Textbook §6.
- **The quantity figures — `dodge it like a` / `even know ya` / `dolla for ya`.** Each is four syllables fired fast off the hit. None is a close rhyme on its own, but the **bulk** — four quick syllables of matched shape, all trailing into a schwa — reads *as* a strong rhyme by **quantity** (thread C). A touch of alliteration seals it: **d**odge / **d**olla. The quantity route of §6 in full flight.
- **The hit that doesn't rhyme — `name`.** *name* binds to no rhyme thread, yet it lands clean. Three things carry it: it sits on a **downbeat** (beat 2); it **alliterates** — *k**now** ya **n**ame* is just satisfying in the mouth; and it's a **late echo** of *shade* two beats up (/eɪ/) — a rhyme that arrives a touch behind where the ear half-expected it (thread D, displaced). Living proof of the doc's claim that **rhyme is the free variable** (§4): a hit only owes the ear a *payoff*, and *name* pays in sound and placement instead of rhyme.

**Placement and pace.** Both lines are `floatOpen · floatLand` — pure drift — gliding in off a pickup (*I can…*, *don't…*) and trailing past the last hit (*dive*, *ride*). And it's **fast**: hits on nearly every beat of bars 2–3, fills packed to the brim. The one pocket of air is the rest right after *shade* (`shade : _ | _ : im : ma`) — a single held breath — and that breath is what makes the rapid-fire after it *pop*. Density only reads as density against a little space (§5).

**Rhyme threads.**

```
A : duck · bus                                   the double's stressed onset — impact, on b4   /ʌ/    perfect
B : dive · ride                                  the trailer riding off A                       /aɪ/   perfect
C : dodge-it-like-a · even-know-ya · dolla-for-ya  quantity figures — bulk reads as rhyme       (schwa)  + dodge/dolla allit.
D : shade · name                                 a displaced, slightly-late echo               /eɪ/   slant
```

Grounding all of it: *feel* and *even* on the 1s — anchorHits that carry no rhyme and don't need to. They pay off by reinforcement, holding the floor under everything exposed and syncopated around them.

### Strengthening the anchorHit — broke-a-sweat (a revision)

**The idea.** The anchor — the downbeat of the 1 — is the slot the ear braces for hardest (§1), so it's the highest-value place to land a rhyme. A hit there is strongest when the **rhyme sits on the impact onset itself**, not buried in the trail behind it (§6). Here's one combo **before and after** that fix: same skeleton, the rhyme dragged onto the anchor.

**Before — the rhyme is buried off the downbeat.**

```
anchor:  || # | _ | _ | _ ||

|| _:_:sit:the | deep:est:in:the | line:up | _:_:ne:ver ||
|| *break:a:sweat=B:_ | _:_:and:you | *know:that:I:be | *pat:ient:I:wait ||
|| *for:the:best=B:_ | _ | _ | _ ||
```
*"Sit the deepest in the lineup, never break a sweat, and you know that I be patient, I wait for the best."*

The two hits on the 1 — *break*, *for* — **don't rhyme**. They pay off only by **quantity**: *break-a-sweat* and *for-the-best* are fast three-syllable figures that land as a unit. But the combo's actual rhyme — *sweat / best* (thread B, /ɛ/) — sits on **slot 3**, *behind* the downbeat. So the strongest slot in the bar carries an unrhymed onset, and the payoff arrives a beat-fraction late, in the trail. The hit lands; it just doesn't land *where the ear is reaching.*

**After — the rhyme moves onto the anchor.**

```
anchor:  || # | _ | _ | _ ||

|| _:_:sit:the | deep:est:in:the | line:up | _:_:hav:e'nt ||
|| *broke=A:a:sweat=B:_ | *yet=C:_:and:you | *know:that:I:been | *wait:in':I:ain't ||
|| *go=A:to:bed=B:_ | *yet=C | _ | _ ||
```
*"Sit the deepest in the lineup, haven't broke a sweat yet, and you know that I been waitin', I ain't go to bed yet."*

Now *broke / go* (thread A, /oʊ/ perfect) rhyme **on the downbeat of the 1** — the rhyme is on the impact onset, exactly where the ear is braced. And the **quantity is kept**: *broke-a-sweat* and *go-to-bed* are still fast triplets, so the anchor hit pays off **both** ways at once — quality on the onset *and* the bulk of the figure (§6). The old buried rhyme even survives, re-tuned to *sweat / bed* (thread B, /ɛ/) on slot 3 — so the combo is now **doubly bound**: on the anchor *and* behind it.

**What actually changed.** One principle, applied: *put the rhyme on the impact onset.* `break → broke`, `for → go`. Everything downstream was rewritten only to *serve* that landing — *never → haven't*, *I be patient → I been waitin'*, *I wait for the best → I ain't go to bed* — so that *broke* and *go* fall on the 1. The anchor onset went from carrying no rhyme (quantity alone) to carrying the combo's primary rhyme (quality **and** quantity). That is the whole strengthening.

**The bonus — the `yet` tag.** The edit also filled two rests with an **add-on bareHit** (§2): *yet* on beat 2, a 16th off each anchor figure (*broke a sweat → yet*, *go to bed → yet*), the same word both lines (thread C). It costs nothing — those slots were empty in the *before* — and adds a recurring exposed snap right behind the strengthened hit. (*know* on beat 3 quietly carries the /oʊ/ too — a third touch of thread A riding a bareHit.)

**Rhyme threads (after).**

```
A : broke · go    on the downbeat of the 1 — the rhyme dragged onto the impact onset   /oʊ/   perfect
B : sweat · bed   the buried rhyme, slot 3, behind the anchor                           /ɛ/    slant
C : yet · yet     add-on bareHit on beat 2 — a repeated exposed hook                    exact  (tag)
```

The lesson in one line: a fast figure can land a hit by **quantity**, but the **anchor** wants the rhyme *on its onset* — that's the difference between a hit that merely lands and a hit that lands where the ear is reaching.

## How this doc sits in the framework

`comboBounce` is the **timing sibling of `lineCombo.md`** — same unit, the rhythm half. It borrows and points; it does not redefine:

- **`calileiNotation.md`** owns the glyphs and laws — `#`/`*`, slots, subdivision, L8 (anchor), L9 (hit / bounce as separate layers), G2/G3. comboBounce is the *theory* G3 promised; it cites these, never re-specs them.
- **`lineCombo.md`** owns what a combo *is* — call → response, the two lines, completeness, the landing, hit-anchored rhyme. comboBounce times that unit; it does not own its meaning.
- **`rhymingWords.md`** owns rhyme *closeness* (perfect / slant / multis). comboBounce only fixes *where in time* the binding onset falls (the stressed downbeat, §6).
- **`prosodyMeter.md`** owns stress and meter in the abstract. comboBounce assumes them and applies them against the pulse.

What comboBounce **leaves for later**:

- **`voiceStyle.md` §6 (Flow & Sound)** — *where this voice* sets these dials: floaty vs. punchy, anchorHit vs. bareHit lean, how dense the fills run. That is idiolect, drawn from the combos, and it **points here** for what the dials are — the same way every other facet points at its craft doc.
- A **settings layer** for each source studied. Drill (anchor on the downbeat of the 1 with a varying period; bareHits gravitating to the 3; the impact onset on the downbeat; dense interior rhyme) is the first; others can be added as the style mines them, without touching the neutral core above.
