## Part 1: Writing vs. Designing

Most advice about lyrics focuses on **writing** — what to say, what story to tell, what emotion to land. This document is about something else: **lyric design**, the craft of arranging sound itself so that a line is satisfying to deliver and pleasurable to hear, largely independent of what it means.

A well-written line communicates. A well-designed line *physically rewards* the mouth that speaks it and the ear that hears it. The best lyrics do both at once — and, crucially, the two reinforce each other: a line that is easy and pleasing to produce is also easier to perceive, remember, and believe. But they are not the same skill, and most aspiring writers neglect design because no one ever names it as a separate discipline.

Phonetic appeal is the engine of design. It rests on two pillars:

1. **The mechanics of sound** — what the mouth is actually doing when it produces speech, and which sequences of movements feel natural versus jagged.
2. **The placement of sonic devices** — alliteration, assonance, rhyme, repetition — used as deliberately structured ornament, not as decoration sprinkled everywhere.

The goal of this document is to make both pillars explicit, give you vocabulary for diagnosing why a line works or doesn't, and supply checklists you can run a draft through.

## Part 2: The One Idea Underneath Everything — Processing Fluency

If you remember one concept, make it this: **processing fluency** — the ease with which a mind takes something in. It is the hidden variable behind nearly every "feels good" judgment in this document.

The remarkable finding from cognitive science is that the features which make a sound sequence **easy to produce** (in the mouth) overlap heavily with the features that make it **easy to perceive and remember** (in the ear and mind) — and that ease of processing is *itself experienced as pleasure, liking, and even truth*. So "feels nice to say," "sounds good to hear," and "rings true" are not three separate axes. They are mostly **one axis** seen from three angles.

The sharpest evidence is the **rhyme-as-reason effect** (the "Keats heuristic," after McGlone and Tofighbakhsh). When people rate aphorisms that are identical in meaning, they judge the **rhyming version as more accurate and truthful** than the non-rhyming one. The rhyme adds fluency, and the mind quietly misattributes that fluency to "this must be right." The same boost shows up for chiastic structures ("All for one and one for all") and for advertising slogans. Repetition produces the same illusion of truth through sheer familiarity.

The practical upshots:

- **Fluency is persuasion.** A well-designed line doesn't just sound nicer; it makes the listener *trust* the sentiment more. This is why slogans, proverbs, and hooks lean so hard on sound.
- **Ease and beauty are the same lever.** When you smooth a line's articulation (Part 4) or tighten its sound-repetition (Part 8), you are simultaneously making it more memorable and more believable.
- **Familiarity breeds liking.** The **mere-exposure effect** means repeated material becomes more pleasant simply by recurring — the engine under choruses and hooks (Part 10).

Hold one caveat alongside this: fluency has a ceiling. Past a certain point, *too* fluent becomes bland and forgettable. The art (Part 11) is a fluent, singable frame with one or two deliberately *striking* elements that snag attention. Fluency gets you liked; a spike of surprise gets you remembered.

## Part 3: The Mechanics of Sound (Articulation)

Every consonant is defined by three things: **where** the sound is made in the vocal tract (place of articulation), **how** the airflow is obstructed (manner of articulation), and whether the vocal cords are vibrating (voicing). Understanding these tells you why some word sequences feel like dancing and others feel like tripping.

### 3.1 Place of Articulation

The mouth has a series of "stations" running from front (lips) to back (throat). Each consonant is produced at one of them:

| Place         | Where                                            | English Examples   |
|  ---          |  ---                                             |  ---               |
| Bilabial      | Both lips                                        | P, B, M            |
| Labiodental   | Lower lip + upper teeth                          | F, V               |
| Dental        | Tongue tip + upper teeth                         | TH (*think, this*) |
| Alveolar      | Tongue tip + ridge behind teeth                  | T, D, S, Z, N, L   |
| Post-alveolar | Tongue + just behind the ridge                   | SH, ZH, CH, J      |
| Palatal       | Tongue body + hard palate                        | Y                  |
| Velar         | Back of tongue + soft palate                     | K, G, NG           |
| Labio-velar   | Lips round *and* tongue body raises at the velum | W                  |
| Glottal       | Vocal folds                                      | H                  |

**Why this matters for lyrics:** when consecutive consonants are at the same or adjacent stations, the mouth makes a small, cheap movement. When they jump across the mouth, the tongue (or the active articulator) has to travel further, which costs effort and time. That cost can read as either a stumble or a deliberate emphasis. Both effects are tools — you just have to know which one you're deploying.

A sequence like `K → T` (velar → alveolar) is a small frontward hop. A sequence like `K → P` (velar → bilabial) is a bigger leap from back to front. Say *kit* and then *kip* slowly: the second word's closing gesture travels further, and you can feel it.

### 3.2 Manner of Articulation

How the airflow is shaped:

- **Stops / plosives** (P, B, T, D, K, G) — air is fully blocked, then released in a burst. These are your **percussion**. They punch. A stop is literally a tiny silence followed by a transient — an attack.
- **Fricatives** (F, V, S, Z, SH, ZH, TH, H) — air is squeezed through a narrow gap, making a sustained hiss. These are **texture**. Sibilants (S, SH, Z) cut and hiss; F, V, and H whisper.
- **Affricates** (CH, J) — a stop released into a fricative, in one motion. Sharp and rich.
- **Nasals** (M, N, NG) — air resonates through the nose. These **hum and sustain**; you can hold them.
- **Liquids** (L, R) — partial obstruction, voiced, sustainable. These **flow and link**.
- **Glides / approximants** (W, Y) — barely obstructed. Smooth ramps into vowels.

The single most useful way to hear this taxonomy is by **role in the bar**:

| Role                    | Sounds                  | Use on…                                                  |
|---                      |---                      |---                                                       |
| **Percussion / attack** | stops, affricates       | downbeats, accents, the snap at the end of a punchy line |
| **Sustain / legato**    | vowels, nasals, liquids | held notes, melisma, the connective tissue between hits  |
| **Texture / air**       | fricatives, sibilants   | color, hiss, breathiness, menace — used sparingly        |

A line built only of fricatives sounds whispered; a line built only of stops sounds machine-gunned. The mix is the music.

### 3.3 Voicing

A voiced consonant uses vocal-fold vibration (B, D, G, V, Z, J, M, N, L, R, W, Y). A voiceless one doesn't (P, T, K, F, S, SH, CH, H, TH-as-in-*think*). Voiceless sounds are **sharper, brighter, quicker**; voiced sounds are **warmer, rounder, slower**.

Most stops and many fricatives come in voiced/voiceless pairs at the same place: P/B (bilabial), T/D (alveolar), K/G (velar), F/V (labiodental), S/Z (alveolar). You can preserve the *mouth shape* of a line while swapping voicing to change its temperature. *Pat* and *bad* share an articulatory feel; *pat* is harder and brighter, *bad* is rounder and warmer. (This isn't an accident — see the frequency code in Part 5: voicing lowers a sound's spectral energy, which reads as larger, darker, softer.)

### 3.4 Vowels

Vowels carry the **pitch, the melody, and most of the acoustic energy** of any line. They are described by tongue and lip posture:

- **High / Low** — how close the tongue is to the roof of the mouth (*beat* /i/ is high; *bat* /æ/ is low).
- **Front / Back** — whether the tongue is forward or pulled back (*see* /i/ is front; *sue* /u/ is back).
- **Rounded / Unrounded** — whether the lips purse. In English, back vowels (*oo, oh, aw*) are rounded; front vowels (*ee, ay, eh*) are unrounded. (Rounding mostly rides along with backness in English, so there's no perfectly clean rounding-only minimal pair — which is itself worth knowing.)

Short vowels (*ɪ, ɛ, æ, ʌ, ʊ*) are snappy; long vowels and diphthongs (*ee, oo, eye, oh, ay, ow*) sustain. Front vowels feel **brighter, smaller, sharper**; back/low vowels feel **darker, bigger, more open**.

**Why this matters for lyrics:** vowel choice is the closest thing you have to writing melody with the mouth itself. A line that returns to one vowel across its stressed positions feels **anchored**; a line that swings widely between vowel positions feels like **motion**. And because sustained pitch lives on vowels, vowel choice is the single biggest factor in how a line *sings* (Part 6).

### 3.5 The Articulatory Pathway

Reading a lyric, the mouth executes an unbroken sequence of postures. Three checks help diagnose flow:

1. **Articulatory distance and overlap (re-framed from v1's "direction of travel").** The real cost isn't *direction* per se — it's *how far the articulators travel* and *whether neighboring gestures can overlap*. Adjacent or compatible sounds can be **co-articulated** (started before the previous one finishes), which is what makes a sequence glide. Large place jumps and incompatible gestures can't overlap, so they cost time and effort. A *consistent* sweep (say, generally back-to-front) tends to feel smooth — but that's because a monotonic sweep happens to minimize big back-and-forth excursions, not because the mouth has a preferred compass direction. Diagnose for *distance and overlap*, and the "smooth sweep" intuition falls out for free.

2. **Cluster sonority.** Within a single syllable, the easiest onsets *rise* in sonority toward the vowel: stop → liquid → vowel (CL, PL, BL, GL, FL, SL, TR, DR, PR, CR, GR…). These are easy across the world's languages. Clusters that *don't* rise smoothly (KT, PT) are harder. (Full treatment in Part 4.)

3. **Cross-syllable junctions.** Even if each syllable is fine, the *seam* between them matters. Ending one syllable on a stop and starting the next on another stop ("act-ed") forces a complete airflow reset. Ending on a liquid or vowel and starting on a vowel glides straight through.

## Part 4: Sequencing for Flow vs. Friction

A handful of patterns recur in lines that performers call "buttery" — and a handful create the opposite, which you can use on purpose.

### 4.1 The Sonority Hierarchy

**Sonority** is roughly the inherent loudness/openness of a sound. From most to least sonorous:

> **vowels > glides (W, Y) > liquids (L, R) > nasals (M, N, NG) > fricatives > stops**
> *(within the obstruents, voiced sounds are slightly more sonorous than their voiceless twins.)*

This single ranking governs how syllables are shaped and which clusters feel easy.

### 4.2 The Sonority Sequencing Principle (the law of the syllable)

The near-universal pattern: **sonority rises into the vowel, then falls after it.** The vowel is the loudness peak; everything ramps up to it and back down.

*plant* → **p**(stop, low) **l**(liquid, higher) **a**(vowel, peak) **n**(nasal) **t**(stop). A clean rise to the peak and a clean fall away.

A made-up onset like *tlap* is impossible in English because **tl** doesn't rise smoothly into the vowel. When you design a line, prefer onsets that respect the rise. **Stop + liquid** (CL, BL, FL, SL, PL, GL, TR, DR, PR, CR, GR…) is the workhorse cluster of English and feels effortless.

**The famous exception:** **S + stop** (SP, ST, SK, and extended SPR/STR/SKR). These technically *violate* the rise — /s/ is more sonorous than the following stop — yet they feel completely fluent. Linguists treat the /s/ as a kind of "appendix" that sits outside the normal syllable rules. Practically: trust SP/ST/SK; they sound great.

### 4.3 CV Alternation and the Babble Default

The cleanest possible speech is **consonant–vowel–consonant–vowel (CVCV)**: the mouth simply alternates between closing and opening, with no traffic jam. This is the *most basic, lowest-effort rhythm the human mouth makes* — it falls out of the natural opening-and-closing cycle of the jaw, which is why it dominates the babbling of every baby in every language, and why the first words across languages are CV reduplications: **mama, papa, dada, baba**. (Bilabials + the open vowel /a/ are the earliest, easiest sounds; almost no language lacks them.)

Two direct consequences for songwriting:

- **Reduplication is the most singable shape there is.** *na-na-na, la-la-la, oh-oh, doo-doo, hey-hey* — these are pure CV repetition, maximally easy to produce, language-independent, and minimal in semantic load. This is *why* the most viral hooks are built from vocables. (Real-world touchstones, described not quoted: the wordless "la"-type chant that carries a Lady Gaga hook; the one-word title that another pop hit loops by detaching and repeating its final syllable.)
- **Dense consonant clusters cut against it.** Runs like CCCVCC (*strengths*, *twelfths*) crowd the mouth. A crowded mouth is a *tense* mouth — useful for effort, grit, or agitation, but it slows delivery. Choose it; don't stumble into it.

### 4.4 Repeating Mouth Shapes

Lines feel cohesive when consecutive stressed positions repeat *mouth-shape information* — same place, same manner, or same vowel — even when the exact sounds differ. The mouth keeps doing **similar work**, which the ear reads as unity.

This is the key insight under *all* the named literary devices in Part 8: alliteration, assonance, consonance, and rhyme are simply **different granularities of getting the mouth to repeat itself**. Naming them separately is useful for placement; underneath, they're one phenomenon.

### 4.5 The Arc (v1's "U-Shape")

Many memorable short hooks trace a deliberate **sonic arc**: begin at one place in the mouth, swing out through an open vowel, and land at a contrasting place. A three-syllable phrase that goes **back → open → front** (velar onset, low open vowel, bilabial or front close) is a complete little physical journey — and a mouth that has just completed a satisfying journey *wants to repeat it*, which is half of what "quotable" means. Treat the arc as one option in your kit, not a law; it's a way to give a tiny phrase a feeling of shape and closure.

## Part 5: Sound That Carries Meaning (Phonosemantics)

This is the deepest lever in the whole document, and v1 only grazed it with "bright vs. dark vowels." Sounds are **not** a neutral envelope for meaning. Many of them *carry* meaning on their own, below the level of the dictionary. Used well, this lets you make a line's **sound agree with its sense** — which is the most powerful, least obvious form of "psychology of word choice."

### 5.1 The bouba/kiki effect

Show people two shapes — one rounded blob, one jagged star — and two nonsense words, *bouba* and *kiki*. Across cultures and writing systems, people overwhelmingly call the blob *bouba* and the star *kiki*. The reason is acoustic and articulatory: **voiced sounds and rounded back vowels carry low-frequency energy and read as round, soft, large**; **voiceless stops and high front vowels carry high-frequency energy and read as sharp, spiky, small**. The mapping is real and largely pre-linguistic.

### 5.2 The Frequency Code (the master tool)

Ohala's **frequency code** generalizes bouba/kiki into a single, usable rule:

> **The characteristic acoustic frequency of a speech sound maps *inversely* onto perceived size — and, by extension, onto brightness, sharpness, speed, and lightness vs. their opposites.**

- **Small / bright / sharp / quick / light** → **high front vowels** (*ee /i/, ih /ɪ/, ay /eɪ/, eh /ɛ/*) + **voiceless / sharp consonants** (*t, k, p, s*) + high pitch.
- **Big / dark / heavy / slow / looming** → **low back vowels** (*ah /ɑ/, aw /ɔ/, oh /oʊ/, oo /u/*) + **voiced sonorants** (*m, n, l, r, b, g, d*) + low pitch.

This isn't arbitrary. Sapir's classic experiment offered people nonsense words like *mil* and *mal* and asked which named a bigger table: people chose the /a/-word for "large" up to ~96% of the time. Biologically, big bodies have long vocal tracts that resonate low; small bodies resonate high — and animals (including us) exploit this, dropping pitch to seem threatening and raising it to seem small and harmless. Your listener carries that instinct.

**How to use it:** you can tune a line's emotional temperature *purely through vowel and consonant selection*, independent of what the words denote. Want a lyric to feel tiny, glittering, and fast? Load it with *ee/ih* and *t/k/s*. Want it to feel vast, slow, and ominous? Load it with *oh/aw/oo* and *m/n/l/r*. When sound and sense agree, fluency spikes and the line feels *inevitable*. (See Worked Example 2.)

### 5.3 Phonesthemes (sound clusters with built-in meaning)

A **phonestheme** is a sub-word sound chunk that carries a recurring meaning without being a true prefix or suffix. English is dense with them. They're *tendencies*, not rules (counterexamples always exist), but they exert a real pull:

| Cluster | Tends to mean | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **gl-** | light, vision, shine | glow, gleam, glint, glitter, glisten, glance, glare |
| **sl-** | slippery / pejorative | slime, slug, slush, sloppy, slick, slither, slink |
| **sn-** | nose, contempt | sniff, snore, snout, sneer, snarl, snub, sneeze |
| **fl-** | flickering light / motion | flicker, flutter, flap, flit, flash, flare, flow |
| **gr-** | low, harsh sound / displeasure | growl, grumble, groan, grind, gruff, grim |
| **cr- / -ack** | sudden breakage, impact | crack, crash, crunch, crackle, crack |
| **-ash** | violent contact | crash, smash, bash, splash, clash, dash, lash |
| **-ump** | heavy rounded mass | lump, bump, hump, stump, clump, plump, rump |
| **tw-** | twist, turn | twist, twirl, tweak, twine, twitch |
| **-oom / -oon** | large, resonant (low back vowel) | boom, zoom, gloom, doom, loom |

Threading a phonestheme through the load-bearing words of a line creates a **subterranean unity** the listener feels without being able to name — and if you pick a phonestheme that matches your meaning, sound and sense reinforce each other.

### 5.4 Ideophones and onomatopoeia

The strongest form of sound-meaning is **onomatopoeia / ideophones** — words that *are* their sound or sensation. English has plenty (*buzz, hiss, thud, click, splash, murmur*), and some languages are extraordinarily rich in them; Japanese mimetics are the textbook case (*kirakira* = a sparkle, *dokidoki* = a pounding heart, *fuwafuwa* = soft and fluffy). When you need maximum sound-sense fusion, reach for the ideophone.

### 5.5 An honest caveat

Judgments about which whole *languages* or words are "beautiful" are heavily contaminated by **familiarity and even cultural prejudice** — "euphony" has often been a proxy for what a listener was raised on. So: the *specific, acoustically-grounded* effects above (bouba/kiki, the frequency code, phonesthemes) are robust and cross-cultural and worth using. But don't over-essentialize "ugly" vs. "pretty" sounds in the abstract. In practice, **fit-to-meaning** and **freshness** matter as much as any raw sonic quality.

## Part 6: Speaking vs. Singing — The Sung Line

v1 is, at heart, a guide to **spoken and rapped** delivery — its examples snap and punch. That's a complete craft on its own. But the moment a lyric is **sung**, an extra layer of physics kicks in, and ignoring it is the most common reason a lyric that *reads* great *sings* badly.

### 6.1 Pitch lives on vowels

When you sustain a note, you are sustaining a **vowel**. The vocal tract is most open during vowels, so they carry the pitch, the volume, and nearly all the tone. **Consonants interrupt the airflow** — they're the rhythmic punctuation *between* sung pitches, not the thing being sung. A held note is a held vowel with consonants clipped onto its front and back.

### 6.2 The open-vowel rule (the "money note")

**Put your biggest, highest, longest, most-belted notes on open vowels** — *ah /ɑ/, aw /ɔ/, "a" /æ/, "ay" /eɪ/*, and to a degree *oh /oʊ/* and *eye /aɪ/*. **Avoid landing them on close vowels** — *ee /i/* and *oo /u/* — which are physically harder to sing powerfully or high; the narrow mouth shape fights resonance and strains the voice.

This is not folklore. Acoustic modeling of belting shows that for most vowels the full belt tone is only available over about a musical fifth, but on the open vowels **/ɑ/ and /ɔ/ that range stretches to a full octave.** The "money note" of a great chorus is almost always engineered — consciously or by ear — so its climactic syllable lands on an open vowel. If your hook peaks on an *ee* or *oo*, consider rewriting the word.

### 6.3 What singers do when the lyric won't cooperate

- **Vowel modification.** As pitch rises, singers subtly reshape vowels toward more singable, resonant shapes. A high note written on *ee* will get colored toward *ih* or *eh* whether you like it or not — so anticipate it.
- **Diphthong handling.** Words like *my, time, now, day* contain two vowels. Singers **sustain the first vowel** and add the glide only at the last instant (*ah…→ee* for *my*), because pure monophthongs hold a steadier tone. If you put a long held note on a diphthong, know that the singer is really holding its first half.

### 6.4 Consonants as rhythmic articulation in a sung line

In melisma (many notes on one syllable) the consonants vanish and you're riding a pure vowel. Everywhere else, consonants are the **rhythmic onsets** that place each note in time — which means **stops** still give crisp rhythmic attack and **liquids/nasals** still glue notes together, exactly as in speech. The difference is only that they no longer carry the sustain.

> **Design implication:** decide *early* whether a line is meant to be rapped, spoken, or sung — and if sung, where its long notes fall. Then choose the words that put open vowels under the long notes and crisp consonants on the rhythmic hits.

## Part 7: Prosody — Matching Stress to Beat

This is the other half of "feels good to say," and v1 didn't cover it. **Prosody**, in the songwriting sense, is the *fit between the natural rhythm of language and the rhythm of the music.* Get it right and the line sounds like a real person talking or singing; get it wrong and the line sounds translated, hurried, or fake — no matter how clever its sounds are.

### 7.1 Stressed syllables want strong beats

Every English word has a built-in **stress pattern**: re-MEM-ber, GUI-tar, COME-back, BEAU-ti-ful. Music has **strong and weak positions** (in 4/4, beat 1 is strongest, then 3, then 2 and 4, then the off-beats). The core rule:

> **Align stressed syllables with strong musical positions, and unstressed syllables with weak ones.** A song is, in the end, *natural speech, exaggerated.*

When you violate this to cram words onto a melody — putting a strong beat on an unstressed syllable — you create what we'll call a **greedy spot**: the line speeds up unnaturally, the word gets mispronounced, and the listener loses the illusion of a real voice. The emotion leaks out.

**Illustration (4/4; CAPS = the syllable forced onto a strong beat):**

```
Line: "Do you remember the rain?"

GOOD   beat:  (1)        2     3    (4)        1
       text:  do you   re-MEM-ber  the        RAIN
       → strong beats land on MEM and RAIN, the syllables speech already stresses.

BAD    beat:   1     2     3      4         1
       text:   DO   you   RE-mem-BER  the   RAIN
       → strong beats fall on "DO," "RE-," and "-BER," wrenching the word.
         Sounds robotic and hurried.
```

### 7.2 Use mismatch on purpose: syncopation as a spotlight

The rule has a powerful exception: once you've established a clean, expected match, you can **deliberately break it on a single word to spotlight it.** Dragging or syncopating across a word like *slow* to enact slowness, or hitting an off-beat to make a word jump out — these work *precisely because* the surrounding lines are well-matched. Establish the pattern, then violate it once, meaningfully. (This is the rhythmic cousin of "negative space," Part 12.)

### 7.3 Stable vs. unstable: the master lens

A single question organizes an enormous amount of prosodic choice: **is this idea resolved or unresolved — and does the form make the listener feel that?** Tools for stability and instability:

| Feels **stable / resolved**       | Feels **unstable / wanting more**     |
|---                                |---                                    |
| Even number of lines (2, 4)       | Odd number / a line repeated 3×       |
| ABAB rhyme scheme                 | ABBA, or a withheld rhyme             |
| Perfect rhyme                     | Slant rhyme, assonance                |
| Phrase lands on the downbeat      | Phrase starts after the downbeat      |
| Line ends on a stressed beat      | Line ends on a weak beat / trails off |
| Closed final syllable / hard stop | Open final vowel left hanging         |

Match the form's stability to the emotion. A line that should feel settled gets even phrasing and a perfect rhyme on the downbeat; a line that should feel anxious, yearning, or unfinished gets the unstable versions. (See Worked Example 6.)

## Part 8: The Devices, by Layer

This is v1's strongest structural idea, kept and expanded. Sonic devices operate at different **layers** of language. **Combining devices from different layers** gives you density without redundancy; **stacking devices on the same layer** gives you noise.

### Layer 1 — Sub-syllabic (consonants and vowels)

- **Alliteration** — initial-consonant repetition: *slap, slip, slope*.
- **Consonance** — consonant repetition *anywhere* in the word: *pack / clip / luck* (K's in various positions).
- **Assonance** — vowel repetition: *cold cup, slow sip*.
- **Sibilance** — specifically S/SH/Z repetition for hiss texture.
- **Plosive emphasis** — clustering stops on stressed beats for punch.

### Layer 2 — Syllable (rhyme)

- **Perfect rhyme** — stressed vowel + everything after it matches: *night / light*.
- **Family rhyme** *(new)* — vowels match; final consonants are merely in the *same articulatory family* (e.g., the voiced stops B/D/G, or the nasals M/N/NG): *rib / rig*, *home / hone*. Slightly less resolved than perfect rhyme — a useful in-between.
- **Slant / near rhyme** — close but not identical (matching vowels *or* consonants): *middle / little*, *shape / fate*.
- **Assonance rhyme** — only the vowels match; consonants differ: *lake / shade*.
- **Multisyllabic rhyme ("multis")** — two or more syllables match: *agony / battery*.
- **Mosaic rhyme** — the rhyme is assembled across word boundaries: *poet / know it*.

(Full treatment in Part 9 — rhyme is a *graded* tool, not just a list.)

### Layer 3 — Word and phrase (lexical repetition)

- **Anaphora** — repeating a word/phrase at the *start* of consecutive lines.
- **Epistrophe** — repeating it at the *end* of consecutive lines.
- **Refrain / hook word** — a word that returns at structurally important positions. *This is not alliteration* — it operates on a different layer (meaning recurring, not a sound being beaten).
- **Mirror phrasing / parallelism** — the same syntactic shape across lines.
- **Chiasmus / antimetabole** — the same words reversed ("ask not what your country…"). Highly fluent and memorable.

### Layer 4 — Line and verse (structural)

- **Couplet pairing** — every two lines speak to each other.
- **Caesura** — a deliberate mid-line pause.
- **Line-length variation** — a short line for emphasis, a long line for setup.

> **The cardinal rule of layering:** combine devices from *different* layers. Alliteration + word repetition + slant rhyme = three independent dimensions of interest, none fighting the others. Alliteration + alliteration + alliteration = one dimension hammered to death.

## Part 9: Rhyme in Depth

Rhyme deserves its own section because it does two jobs at once: it satisfies **prediction-and-reward** (the ear anticipates the return of a sound and is rewarded when it lands) *and* it pumps **processing fluency** (which, per Part 2, the mind reads as truth and pleasure — the Keats heuristic in action). That double action is why rhyme is so disproportionately powerful in song.

### 9.1 Rhyme as a graded scale of closeness

Stop thinking of rhyme as on/off. Think of a **dial** running from maximally closed to maximally open, and turn it to match how *resolved* the moment should feel:

```
MOST CLOSED / RESOLVED                                 MOST OPEN / UNRESOLVED
perfect  →  family  →  slant  →  assonance  →  consonance  →  (no rhyme)
```

- **Perfect / family rhyme** support **stable, resolved, settled** meaning — the click of a closing door, the punchline, the vow.
- **Slant / assonance / consonance** support **unstable, yearning, conversational, unfinished** meaning — and as a bonus they unlock a vastly larger vocabulary, which is why modern pop, R&B, and rap lean on them so heavily.

The craft move: when a line *should* feel resolved but you're forcing a perfect rhyme that warps the wording, the warp costs you more than the rhyme buys. Loosen to a slant rhyme and let the language stay natural.

### 9.2 Rhyme position

- **End rhyme** — at line ends; the default skeleton.
- **Internal rhyme ("inners")** — *within* a line; adds density and momentum without committing the line-end.
- **Holorhyme / chained rhyme** — dense webs where rhymes recur mid- and end-line.

### 9.3 The rap toolkit: multis and density

In rap especially, rhyme richness is a marker of skill. **Multisyllabic rhyme** matches a *sequence* of stressed vowels across phrases while letting the consonants differ — the ear hears the recurring **vowel melody** as rhyme. Skilled writers will even bend a word's natural stress to make a multi land harder (a deliberate, controlled version of the Part 7 "greedy spot"). There's even computational evidence that **denser multisyllabic rhyme correlates with what listeners rate as more skillful.** (Eminem is the usual touchstone for multi density — referenced, not quoted.)

### 9.4 Why "wrong" rhymes can be right

A *withheld* or *deferred* rhyme (you set up an expectation, then make the listener wait, or never resolve it) creates tension and forward pull. Use it where the emotion is unresolved. A rhyme that arrives *too* predictably can feel nursery-ish — fine for a singalong, deadly for a confession.

## Part 10: Repetition and Catchiness

"Catchy" is not mystical. The research on **earworms** (involuntary musical imagery) — which over 90% of people experience — points to concrete, reproducible features. Most are melodic, but several are squarely the lyricist's business.

- **Contiguous repetition is the single most earworm-inducing feature.** A fragment that repeats *immediately, without delay* — back-to-back — sticks hardest. Don't space your hook out; loop it. The chorus that repeats its core phrase right away out-sticks the one that scatters it.
- **Make the hook easy to sing.** Earworm-prone tunes have **longer notes and smaller pitch intervals** (easy to reproduce in the head) and a **familiar overall contour with one distinctive twist.** Lyrically, that means: open vowels under the long notes (Part 6), low articulatory load (Part 4), and a shape that's mostly expected with one surprise.
- **Mere exposure does real work.** Familiarity *is* liking. A hook earns affection partly by simply recurring — which is the deepest argument for a strong, repeated chorus.
- **The rule of three.** Three repetitions is the sweet spot: enough to imprint, not yet enough to exhaust. (Note Part 7: an *odd* number of repeats also reads as slightly *unstable* — wanting one more — which can be used for tension.)
- **Anchor words.** *(v1's insight, kept.)* A single returning **word** — not a rhyme, not an alliteration — can be the central figure of a hook. Whole-word repetition processes as *meaning coming back*, not *sound being beaten*, so it doesn't fatigue the ear the way over-alliteration does. You can build a whole verse around one anchor word used three or four times while everything around it does different sonic work.
- **Rhetorical repetition schemes.** Anaphora, epistrophe, and chiasmus all compound the fluency advantage and are reliable hook-builders.

## Part 11: The Psychology of Word Choice

Beyond pure sound, *which words* you choose changes how a line lands, sticks, and moves people. Three well-established effects should drive your lexical decisions.

### 11.1 Concreteness and imageability (the "show, don't tell" mechanism)

Concrete, picturable words (*kettle, rust, headlights, gravel*) are remembered far better than abstractions (*sadness, freedom, journey, pain*). The mechanism is **dual coding** (Paivio): an imageable word is stored *twice* — once as language, once as a mental image — so it has two retrieval routes. Imageability is one of the strongest predictors of whether a word is recalled at all.

This is the cognitive backbone of "show, don't tell," and it explains v1's "abstract noun stuffing" failure mode. **Action verbs** are the "power amplifier" of language — *shoulder, splinter, slam, drift, ache* outperform flat verbs and outperform abstract nouns. The rule of thumb: **let the listener infer the emotion from a concrete image rather than naming the emotion.** "The kettle's gone cold" beats "I feel lonely."

### 11.2 Emotional valence and arousal

Emotionally charged words — positive *or* negative — are remembered better than neutral ones, and higher-arousal words land harder. *Chocolate* and *murder* both out-stick *status*. Combined with concreteness, emotion lands hardest of all: a vivid, picturable, emotionally loaded word is the stickiest unit you can write.

### 11.3 Frequency vs. defamiliarization (the fluency/surprise trade-off)

- **High-frequency, common words** process fastest and feel natural and singable — which is exactly why hooks overwhelmingly use simple words, plus direct address (*you*) for intimacy.
- But total familiarity is forgettable. The **isolation effect** (von Restorff) says a *distinctive* item in a familiar frame is the one that's remembered; the literary idea of **defamiliarization** (making the ordinary strange) says a fresh word or image renews attention.

The synthesis ties back to Part 2: **keep the structure fluent and singable, but plant one or two specific, surprising, concrete, sonically-rich words** that snag the memory. Fluency gets you liked; the spike of surprise gets you remembered. A hook of all-common words with one unexpected, concrete, well-sounded word in the key position is the pop ideal.

## Part 12: The Discipline of Placement

This is the part that most distinguishes design from writing — and it's where v1 is excellent. Devices are not decorations to sprinkle uniformly. They are **weight**, and weight has to be *placed*.

### 12.1 Negative space

The unmarked words in a line are not filler. They are the **runway** that lets the marked words take off. A neutral, texture-free opening is what makes a clustered, loaded payoff *pop* when it arrives. (Real-world touchstone, described: a well-known dance-pop hook keeps its opening words deliberately plain, then crowds the back half of the long line with repeated nasal consonants — the plain runway is what makes the cluster land.)

If every word is loaded, none of them registers. **The signal needs noise around it to read as signal.** This is the single most common mistake in amateur lyrics: trying to make *every* word "work." Every word working = no word standing out.

### 12.2 Devices placed, not spread

Decide *where* in the line you want the impact, then put the device **there.** Not before. Not throughout. *There.* A strong line often has:

- a neutral opening (runway),
- a loaded middle or back half (where one device clusters),
- a clean payoff (where a single rhyme or stop lands).

Three different things at three different positions beats the same thing smeared across the whole line.

### 12.3 Density isn't depth

One well-placed sonic effect outperforms five competing ones. When tempted to add a device, ask: *is it doing work the existing devices aren't?* If it's on the **same layer** as something already present, you're probably *weakening* the original. The hardest skill in this entire craft is **leaving things plain.**

### 12.4 Put the heaviest sound where the meaning peaks

The loudest sonic moment of a line should coincide with its most important word. When the **sound climax** and the **meaning climax** land together, the line feels inevitable; when they're misaligned, the ear is pulled one way and the mind another.

## Part 13: Worked Examples (original)

Every line below is written for this guide so it isolates one technique. Build your own the same way: pick the principle first, then engineer a line that demonstrates *only* that.

### Example 1 — Plosive punch + short-vowel anchor (spoken/rapped)

> **"Snap the lock, pack the box, knock it back."**

- **Vowel anchor:** low/back short vowels throughout — *snap /æ/, lock /ɒ/, pack /æ/, box /ɒ/, knock /ɒ/, back /æ/*. The mouth keeps returning to two neighboring vowel postures, so the line feels *anchored*.
- **Consonance (stops everywhere):** *sn-, -ck, p-, -x, kn-, -ck, b-, -ck*. The line is built of plosives — pure percussion.
- **Stop landing:** ends on /k/ (*back*). The line *snaps shut*; nothing trails.
- **Clean CV-ish rhythm:** mostly stop-vowel-stop syllables, no clusters to jam on.
- **Use it for:** aggression, momentum, a punch-in line over a hard beat.

### Example 2 — The frequency code, two ways (sound matching sense)

Same neutral content — *"something moved and made a sound"* — rendered twice:

> **Small / bright / quick:** *"a thin little flicker, a quick silver tick"*

- High front vowels (*ih /ɪ/, ih, i*), voiceless/sharp consonants (*th, l, fl, k, s, lv, t, k*), and the **fl-** phonestheme (*flicker* = flickering light). It *sounds* tiny and fast because every sonic choice agrees with "small."

> **Big / dark / slow:** *"a low rolling rumble that moans and rolls on"*

- Low back vowels (*oh /oʊ/, ʌ, oʊ, oʊ*), voiced sonorants (*l, r, l, r, m, b, l, m, n, r, l, n*), and the **gr-/-umble** phonestheme (*rumble* ≈ a low, displeasing sound). It *sounds* vast and slow.
- **The lesson:** the dictionary meaning barely changed; the *vowel and consonant selection* did all the emotional work. When sound agrees with sense, the line feels inevitable.

### Example 3 — The sung "money note" (open vowel under the big note)

Two candidate chorus endings, both meaning roughly *"I'm staying":*

- **Weak to sing:** a hook that climbs to its highest, longest note on the word *…leave* (/i/). The close front vowel strains and thins at the top.
- **Strong to sing:** the same climb landing on *…stay* (/eɪ/) or *…all* (/ɔ/) or *…go* (/oʊ/) — an open vowel that opens up, rings, and belts.
- **Design move:** write the *line* so the syllable carrying the climactic note is an open vowel. If your draft peaks on an *ee* or *oo*, swap the word. (And remember: if the note's on a diphthong like *stay*, the singer holds the *first* half, *eh→*, and clips the glide on at the end.)

### Example 4 — Multisyllabic / vowel-chain rhyme (rap)

> **"…left the city fading out" / "…felt the pity, shaded doubt"**

- The end units **"fading out" / "shaded doubt"** share the *vowel chain* **/eɪ – aʊ/** while their consonants differ (*f-d-ng* vs. *sh-d-d-t*). The ear processes the recurring vowel melody as a rhyme even though no single word "rhymes" in the schoolbook sense.
- There's also an internal echo (*city / pity*), so the couplet rhymes on two planes at once — density via *different positions* (Layer 2, internal + end), not via stacking the same device.
- **Use it for:** showing verbal control; building forward momentum across long lines.

### Example 5 — Layering done right (three layers, each placed once)

> **"You talked all night, said nothing at all —**
> **now the lock clicks shut down the empty hall."**

- **Layer 1 (sub-syllabic):** Line 1 anchors hard on **/ɔ/** — *talked, all, all* — which also doubles as **lexical repetition** of the word *all*. Line 2 clusters its consonants in the *middle* (*lock clicks shut* — stops + the K-echo) and keeps the opening ("now the") plain.
- **Layer 2 (rhyme):** *all / hall* — a **perfect** rhyme, chosen because the moment is *resolved* (the relationship is over; the door is shut). And *hall* returns to the **/ɔ/** anchor from line 1, so the rhyme and the vowel anchor are the same color — maximum cohesion.
- **Layer 3 (structural):** a syntactic turn from *"you talked…"* (the past, the other person) to *"now…"* (the present, the aftermath). The shape carries the story.
- **Placement:** neutral runway → mid-line consonant cluster → perfect-rhyme payoff on the downbeat. Three different things at three positions; none fighting.

### Example 6 — Before / after (a fix, diagnosed)

> **Before:** *"The sadness I feel is too real to conceal."*

What's wrong:
- **Abstraction:** *sadness* is a concept, not an image — low imageability, lands soft (Part 11).
- **Telling, not showing:** it *names* the emotion instead of evoking it.
- **Over-resolved rhyme:** *feel / real / conceal* is a three-way perfect rhyme — sing-songy, nursery-ish, more closure than the unresolved feeling deserves (Part 9).
- **No texture, no anchor, soft endings.**

> **After:** *"The kettle's gone cold and the door's still open wide."*

Why it's better:
- **Concrete, picturable images** (*kettle, cold, door*) let the listener *infer* the loneliness — dual-coded and far stickier.
- **Unresolved on purpose:** it ends on the open vowel **/aɪ/** (*wide*) with *no* rhyme yet — leaving the feeling hanging, which *matches* a lingering sadness (Part 7's stable/unstable).
- **Sound-sense fit:** the long open vowels and soft sonorants (*cold, door, open, wide*) move slowly, like the mood.
- **The emotion is stronger precisely because it's never named.**

### Example 7 — Reduplication hook (the babble default)

> **"oh-oh, here we go-oh"**

- Pure **CV reduplication** (*oh-oh… go-oh*) on open vowels: the lowest-effort, most-singable, most language-independent shape there is (Part 4.3) — and **contiguous repetition** (Part 10), the top earworm feature.
- It carries almost no semantic load, which is the point: a vocable chant is a *pure* fluency object. Bolt it to the front or back of a chorus as the part everyone shouts.

## Part 14: Pre-Writing — Choose Your Anchors

Before drafting a line or hook, make these decisions on purpose:

1. **Delivery.** Rapped, spoken, or sung? If sung, *where do the long notes fall* — and what open vowels will sit under them? (Part 6.)
2. **The vowel anchor.** One or two vowels you'll return to on stressed positions. Short vowels (*æ, ɪ, ʌ, ɛ*) for snap; long vowels and diphthongs (*eɪ, oʊ, aɪ, u*) for sustain. (Part 3.4.)
3. **The consonant family.** A manner (stops? liquids?) and/or place (front? back?) that recurs on the *load-bearing* words. (Part 8, Layer 1.)
4. **The emotional resolution → rhyme closeness.** Is this resolved or unresolved? That dictates how far down the rhyme dial you go — perfect for closure, slant for tension. (Part 9.)
5. **Sound-to-sense fit.** What does the line *mean*, and which vowels/consonants/phonesthemes make the sound *agree* with it? (Part 5.)
6. **The hook shape.** What's the one phrase that repeats contiguously, and is it built from singable, open-vowel, low-effort sounds? (Part 10.)

Then write. The anchors don't dictate every word — they tell you which words are doing *structural* work and which are *runway*.

> A line built on `short-i + bilabial stops` might come out: *"flipped the lid, gripped it quick, ripped the strip."* Anchor vowel **/ɪ/**, bilabial **P** scattered across the stressed positions, stop landing.

## Part 15: Checklists

### 15.1 Mouth-mechanics check
- [ ] Where does the mouth start and end? Is there a clear journey, or does it bounce randomly?
- [ ] Any large place jumps (e.g., velar → bilabial) you didn't intend? Can adjacent gestures overlap, or do they force resets?
- [ ] Any clusters that violate the sonority rise? Are they doing useful work, or just tripping the tongue?
- [ ] Does the line end on a **stop** (punch) or a **sustain/open vowel** (open)? Is that the effect you want?

### 15.2 Sound-meaning check *(new)*
- [ ] Do the vowels match the feeling? (High-front = small/bright/quick; low-back = big/dark/slow.)
- [ ] Is there a phonestheme you could thread through the key words to reinforce the sense?
- [ ] Is the *sound climax* sitting on the *meaning climax*?

### 15.3 Sung-delivery check *(new)*
- [ ] Do the longest / highest / belted notes land on **open vowels** (*ɑ, ɔ, æ, eɪ*), not on *ee/oo*?
- [ ] Are crisp consonants on the rhythmic hits and sustain-friendly sounds (vowels, nasals, liquids) under the held notes?
- [ ] Any held note stuck on a diphthong you should be aware of?

### 15.4 Prosody check *(new)*
- [ ] Do stressed syllables land on strong beats and unstressed on weak ones?
- [ ] Any **greedy spots** (a word's stress wrenched to fit the melody)?
- [ ] Where you broke the stress pattern, was it *deliberate* (a syncopated spotlight) or an accident?
- [ ] Does the line's stability (even/odd, downbeat/off-beat, open/closed ending) match how resolved the idea is?

### 15.5 Layering & placement check
- [ ] How many *different* devices are present? How many of the *same*? (Different across layers = good; same on one layer = noise.)
- [ ] What's the negative space? Are plain words *deliberately* holding rhythm so the loud words can speak?
- [ ] Are two devices fighting for the same beat?
- [ ] What's the rhyme — perfect / family / slant — and does its closeness match the emotional resolution?

### 15.6 Word-choice check *(new)*
- [ ] Are the key words **concrete and picturable**, or are you stuffing abstractions?
- [ ] Are you *showing* the emotion through an image rather than *naming* it?
- [ ] Strong action verbs where it counts?
- [ ] One surprising / specific / well-sounded word planted in the key position, inside an otherwise fluent frame?

### 15.7 The out-loud test
- [ ] Say it three times. Does it speed up or slow down on repeat? (Speeding up = fluent; snagging = friction.)
- [ ] Where does your mouth want to stumble — is that a feature or a bug?
- [ ] **Whisper it.** Does the rhythm still work with the pitch stripped away?
- [ ] If it's a hook: do you involuntarily want to say it again? If not, it isn't a hook yet.

## Part 16: Common Failure Modes

**Stacking the same device.** Every word starts with the same consonant; every word ends in *-ack*. One layer carrying all the weight. The ear fatigues and the line stops landing. *Fix:* swap half the device-words for runway words; move a device to a different layer.

**No anchor at all.** Random words that merely fit the meter, with no recurring vowel or consonant. *Fix:* pick one element to repeat, even loosely.

**Abstract-noun stuffing.** Lines built on *pain, struggle, journey, freedom.* Low imageability, soft texture, forgettable. *Fix:* replace with concrete, often monosyllabic, sensory nouns — and let the image imply the feeling.

**Sound fighting sense.** Bright, spiky high-front vowels under a heavy, mournful lyric (or vice versa). The frequency code is working *against* you. *Fix:* re-pick vowels/consonants so the sound agrees with the meaning.

**Closed vowels on the big note.** A soaring sung climax stranded on *ee* or *oo*. It strains and thins. *Fix:* rewrite the word so the climactic note sits on an open vowel.

**Greedy spots.** Forcing a word's stress onto the wrong beat to fit the melody. Sounds hurried, robotic, fake. *Fix:* re-phrase, re-time, or pick a word whose natural stress matches the beat.

**Over-resolved rhyme on an unresolved feeling.** A neat perfect rhyme (or a three-way pile-up) where the emotion is actually open and aching. Sounds nursery-ish. *Fix:* loosen to a slant rhyme; let the language breathe.

**Generic motifs as crutches.** Reaching automatically for genre-default words. *Fix:* if you can swap the word for another genre-default without changing the line's effect, you haven't earned it.

**Soft endings on punch lines.** Ending a line that *wants to hit* on a fricative or liquid blunts it. *Fix:* land on a stop (P, T, K, B, D, G).

**Density mistaken for depth.** Five devices crammed in, none breathing. *Fix:* cut to the one best-placed effect; trust the plainness around it.

**Workshopped feel.** Every word visibly chosen, every device showing its work — the line reads like a homework assignment. *Fix:* leave some words plain on purpose. Trust the listener.

## Part 17: Guiding Principles

A condensed list to keep nearby.

1. **Writing decides what to say; design decides where the sound sits.**
2. **Easy to say = easy to hear = easy to believe.** Fluency is the master variable — and it doubles as persuasion.
3. **The mouth is doing physical work.** Mind articulatory distance and overlap, not a compass direction.
4. **Hard stops punch on the strong beats; liquids, nasals, and vowels carry between.**
5. **Make the sound agree with the sense.** High-front/voiceless = small, bright, quick; low-back/voiced = big, dark, slow.
6. **If it's sung, the long notes ride on open vowels.** Never strand a money note on *ee* or *oo*.
7. **Match stressed syllables to strong beats** — then break the match once, on purpose, to spotlight a word.
8. **Tune rhyme closeness to emotional resolution.** Perfect rhyme closes; slant rhyme leaves it aching.
9. **Layer devices, don't stack them.** Three different things on three layers beats three of the same thing.
10. **Repeat the hook contiguously,** and keep it simple and singable. Familiarity *is* liking.
11. **Choose concrete, picturable, emotionally-charged words.** Show the feeling; don't name it.
12. **Negative space is structural.** Plain words let loud words speak; the signal needs noise to read as signal.
13. **Put the heaviest sound where the meaning peaks.**
14. **Keep the frame fluent, but plant one surprise.** Fluency earns liking; the spike earns memory.
15. **Density isn't depth.** The hardest, highest skill is leaving things plain.
16. **Test by mouth, not by eye.** If it doesn't feel good to say, it won't feel good to hear.

## Appendix A — Consonant Transition Reference

Roughly easiest → hardest. (Harder isn't *forbidden* — it's deliberate tension. It should be chosen, not accidental.)

**Smooth**
- Vowel → liquid → vowel (*I'll* sliding between vowels)
- Stop → liquid → vowel (CL, BL, GR, PR…)
- Same place of articulation across a syllable boundary (*good time*: D→T, both alveolar)
- S + stop (SP, ST, SK) — the recognized fluent exception
- Glide → vowel (W, Y ramping in)

**Workable**
- Stop → vowel → stop (CVC, the basic syllable)
- Fricative → vowel → stop
- Adjacent places hopping in one direction (K → T → P, back-to-front)
- Nasal → vowel (always easy; you can even hum into it)

**Effortful (use on purpose)**
- Stop → stop across a syllable boundary (*act-ed* needs a reset)
- Large back-and-forth place jumps (P → K → P)
- Three-plus consonant clusters where sonority doesn't rise (*sixths, twelfths*)
- Same fricative repeated across a seam (*this song*: S→S slurs)
- Same/adjacent sibilants alternating (S↔SH), the classic tongue-twister engine

## Appendix C — Glossary

- **Alliteration** — repetition of initial consonant sounds.
- **Anaphora / Epistrophe** — repetition of a word/phrase at the start / end of consecutive lines.
- **Assonance** — repetition of vowel sounds.
- **Bouba/kiki effect** — cross-cultural tendency to match round shapes to low-frequency sounds and spiky shapes to high-frequency sounds.
- **Chiasmus / antimetabole** — repeating words in reversed order.
- **Coarticulation** — the overlapping of neighboring speech gestures; the basis of "flow."
- **Concreteness / imageability** — how picturable a word is; high-imageability words are remembered better (dual coding).
- **Consonance** — repetition of consonant sounds anywhere in the word.
- **Contiguous repetition** — a fragment repeated immediately, back-to-back; the strongest earworm feature.
- **Defamiliarization** — making the ordinary strange to renew attention.
- **Family rhyme** — vowels match; final consonants are in the same articulatory family.
- **Frequency code** — acoustic frequency maps inversely to perceived size, and onto bright/sharp vs. dark/heavy.
- **Greedy spot** — a place where a word's natural stress is wrenched to fit the melody.
- **Manner of articulation** — *how* airflow is obstructed (stop, fricative, affricate, nasal, liquid, glide).
- **Mere-exposure effect** — repeated exposure increases liking.
- **Mosaic rhyme** — a rhyme assembled across word boundaries (*poet / know it*).
- **Multisyllabic rhyme ("multis")** — two or more syllables rhyming, often by matching a vowel chain.
- **Phonestheme** — a sub-word sound cluster with a recurring meaning (*gl-*, *sl-*, *-ump*).
- **Place of articulation** — *where* in the vocal tract a consonant is made.
- **Processing fluency** — the ease of taking something in; experienced as pleasure, memorability, and perceived truth.
- **Prosody** — the fit between the rhythm of language and the rhythm of music.
- **Rhyme-as-reason (Keats heuristic)** — rhyming statements are judged more truthful because rhyme adds fluency.
- **Sibilance** — repetition of S/SH/Z hiss.
- **Slant / near rhyme** — close but inexact rhyme.
- **Sonority** — the inherent loudness/openness of a sound.
- **Sonority Sequencing Principle** — sonority rises into the vowel and falls after it.
- **Vocable** — a sung nonsense syllable (*la, na, oh, doo*).
- **Voicing** — whether the vocal folds vibrate during a consonant.
- **Vowel modification** — a singer's adjustment of a vowel for resonance, especially up high.
- **von Restorff / isolation effect** — a distinctive item in a uniform set is best remembered.
